# X12 — The Guide to UX Design Process & Documentation

**Source:** Chris Bank, co-written by Jerry Cao, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 2, 6–8). Original filename: `User Experience/The Guide to UX Design Process and Documentation.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. The source is a historical, vendor-published practice guide, not a current standard.

## Select documents that advance a decision

Use documentation to share understanding and move design forward, rather than equating a large paper trail with good UX (pp. 7–21). Product definition, research, analysis, design, implementation and launch overlap; the process loop returns from testing to understanding (p. 10). The guide explicitly permits selecting only useful methods. Sprint lengths, research two iterations ahead, design one ahead, and a 70/30 time split are examples of team practice, not required schedules (pp. 13–17).

Stakeholder interviews, competitor reviews, workshops, sketches and working-backward press releases can reveal assumptions and alternatives. Internal agreement or a compelling press release does not validate user demand (pp. 14–20). Kano categories and 1–5 feature scores are prioritization aids; the Apple personnel anecdote, illustrative download targets and revenue emphasis do not establish universal success criteria.

## Define purpose, constraints and unknowns

Distinguish the core benefit, the actual product and surrounding service when scope is unclear (pp. 22–25). A kickoff plan can make objectives, deliverables, responsibilities, resources, risks and success explicit (pp. 25–30). Prepare enough context for useful participation; do not promise off-record treatment unless it can be honored. Stakeholder card sorts and visual gut tests expose internal expectations, not representative-user preferences (pp. 27–29).

The Lean Canvas example links problems, solutions, customers, value, measures, costs and revenue (pp. 31–32). Treat each unsupported entry as a hypothesis. Company size alone does not determine which canvas is appropriate. A concept map makes relationships and terminology visible (p. 33); the illustrated relationship between a mountain and China uses an imprecise edge label, showing why the relationship itself needs review, not only a tidy diagram. Feature matrices and exclusions help communicate boundaries (pp. 34–36), but do not authorize silently reducing a requested deliverable.

## Research evidence and interpretation are different

Market and user research complement each other (pp. 37–51); a simple what/why division is incomplete. Segment by characteristics relevant to the task without excluding consequential secondary users or inferring motives from demographics (pp. 40–42). Short, focused surveys can reduce burden, but three minutes/five questions are heuristics, and a biased survey is not automatically better than an explicit unknown (pp. 42–44).

Heuristic evaluation examines interfaces against criteria; usability research observes representative people attempting tasks (pp. 44–48). A favorable expert score does not establish successful use. Set objectives, choose methods, conduct research and synthesize findings. Eight users, three-hour interviews and scorecard formats are examples, not sample-size or duration rules. Analytics can identify patterns for investigation; keyword volume or a changed conversion rate alone does not establish cause or task priority (pp. 48–51). Historical vendor capabilities and percentage anecdotes are not current product facts or forecasts.

## Connect situations to tasks and content

Personas focus decisions when grounded in research (pp. 54–57). Vivid names, psychographic guesses and three-to-five personas are not validation. Label provisional attributes and preserve the evidence behind them. Stories describe a role, action and benefit; job stories emphasize the situation, motivation and desired outcome (pp. 57–59). Neither should prematurely dictate the technical solution.

The p. 58 INVEST list uses **Scalable**. Bill Wake's [original 2003 article](https://xp123.com/invest-in-good-stories-and-smart-tasks/) uses **Small**; his [2016 follow-up](https://xp123.com/small-scalable-stories-in-the-invest-model/) explicitly prefers **Scalable** for the level of discussion. Both primary pages were checked 2026-10-25. Preserve the variant rather than declaring the book simply incorrect. The practical point is appropriately sized, valuable, negotiable and testable interaction, not isolated technical layers or an inflexible word template.

Experience maps combine evidence, a chosen lens, journey stages and takeaways (pp. 59–61). A useful narrative still needs provenance; do not invent emotions or force a happy ending. The task matrix compares frequency across profiles (p. 62). Frequency helps prioritize navigation but does not eliminate rare critical recovery/access tasks. The screenshot contains generic Task 1–4 labels; the prose's travel-search interpretation is not independently visible in that table.

Content inventories expose redundant/outdated/trivial material and missing coverage; include owners, terminology, volume and constraints where useful (pp. 62–63). Low usage alone is not sufficient reason to delete content. Top-task ranking, gap analysis, Kano, QFD, Pareto, cause-effect analysis and FMEA are alternative prioritization techniques (pp. 64–66). Scores and 80/20 claims are aids, not mathematical proof; account for severity and affected people, including smaller groups.

## Prototype the uncertainty, not a compulsory fidelity ladder

Sketches, wireframes, mockups and prototypes emphasize different aspects of a product (pp. 67–82). Fidelity has visual, content and behavioral dimensions. A rough prototype may test structure; a more realistic one may be needed for detailed interaction or content. Neither low nor high fidelity automatically prevents bias or unanswered questions. The source's p. 70 claim that paper cannot link conflicts with linked paper-prototype techniques, while p. 71 overstates how completely high fidelity eliminates ambiguity. Ten-to-twenty rough A/B variations are not an experimental-design requirement (pp. 73–77).

The guide distinguishes holistic user journeys from repeated task steps (pp. 77–79). Other teams may use the terms differently: define the artifact's scope instead of policing the name. Observe the underlying task, such as buying flowers, before digitizing an inefficient current workflow. Assets and style requirements communicate reusable decisions (pp. 79–81). Firefox, Salesforce and Yelp illustrate historical guides; a single font or a particular vendor is not mandatory. Do not delay meaningful prototyping solely to produce every document.

## Trace requirements through behavior and verification

A PRD connects purpose, audience, features, constraints and release criteria; an FSD describes externally observable behavior, supported environments, bounds and errors (pp. 83–93). The Product Hunt examples show a concise goal/audience and a glossary with user types (pp. 85, 87–88). The names PRD/FRD/FSD are not globally standardized. A smaller project can combine the needed information without duplicating it across documents.

Map features to objectives so a proposed cut has visible consequences. Define release criteria early across functionality, usability, reliability, performance and supportability (pp. 88–89), rather than lowering the bar to match whatever has been implemented. A sample field limit is not a requirement for a new project. Team agreement helps coordination but does not guarantee absence of errors. Preserve relevant limits and recovery behavior when expressing requirements through tickets or a wiki (pp. 90–93).

## Internal checks, user testing and launch are separate

Internal use and diaries can reveal problems cheaply, but internal participants do not replace representative users (pp. 93–98). The alpha/acceptance terminology overlaps in this source; report the actual participants, tasks, methods and version. The claim that internal testing approximates 80% of real users is not supported here. Beta recruitment should match relevant use conditions and make feedback actionable; this chapter does not authorize contacting anyone in a local-prototype task.

Launch planning connects audience, goals, owners, channel, content and support (pp. 99–109). FAQs should reflect actual questions; demonstrations should explain a real benefit and clear next action; case studies need actual results (pp. 106–108). Do not fabricate customers, testimonials or performance figures. Monday/Tuesday launch timing, under-two-minute videos, Coin's campaign numbers and “one shot” launch rhetoric are historical/contextual claims, not universal instructions. A launch checklist does not authorize publication of a local demo. Page 110 is vendor promotion.

## Reusable application

For both modes, maintain only enough project context to connect user situation → intended outcome → necessary flow/state → design decision → verification. For discovery, reuse existing evidence and distinguish confirmed requirements, assumptions and open questions. For queries, explain when an artifact helps rather than prescribing a full process. For the demos, put briefs and source rationale beside the projects; keep product UI focused on the user's task.
