# U06 — Task-Centered User Interface Design

**Source:** *Task-Centered User Interface Design: A Practical Introduction*, Clayton Lewis and John Rieman, copyright 1993, 1994 (PDF pp. 1–2, 19–20). Original filename: `User Interface/Task Centred User Interface Design.pdf`. The filename uses “Centred”; the cover uses “Centered.” PDF pp. 1–20 are front matter with Roman folios; from PDF p. 21 onward, subtract 20 for the printed body page. All locators below are **PDF pages** unless explicitly marked printed.

The complete text, including front matter, appendices, exercises and index, has been read. Relevant original-page layouts, ASCII interface examples, tables and formula details were inspected. Reading coverage/evidence is maintained outside the skill.

## Design an entire task, including its boundaries

The foreword treats the interface as the extended means of communication between a person and system, including documentation/training, and warns that a good interface cannot simply be spread over an unsuitable system (pp. 13–18). It mainly addresses cognitive aspects rather than supplying a complete physical-accessibility treatment. Its stereo example shows why locally consistent controls can still fail the complete “turn everything off” job: the power operation differs on one component. Familiarity to an experienced designer does not establish discoverability for a novice.

The process begins with who will do what, chooses representative tasks, borrows appropriate existing patterns, sketches, analyzes, prototypes, tests and iterates, then continues observing/changing after delivery (pp. 21–29). The authors explicitly mean borrowing lawful, appropriate interaction ideas, not plagiarism or asset copying (pp. 23, 47). Their shareware notice retains copyright and describes distribution conditions; it is not a public-domain dedication (pp. 2, 17–19). No payment, redistribution or contact with the authors is required for this design-pro review.

Task descriptions should state an intended result without preselecting the interface solution (pp. 31–45). The traffic-modeling example specifies streets, speed ranges and the result to inspect, rather than saying “select a value from a menu” (p. 34). Specific content reveals problems generic examples conceal: customers' long, similarly prefixed filenames became indistinguishable in a narrow list even though tests with short names passed (pp. 34–35). Preserve the meaningful differences people need to identify the right item.

A complete job includes transitions and sources/destinations of data (pp. 35–36). A phone service could support checking a balance and checking a cleared payment separately while failing the combined flow. A graphics tool could draw well yet make users retype data already available in another file. Ask what triggers the job, where input originates, what must happen next, and what constitutes a useful result. A feature checklist complements this work; it does not replace it.

Describe relevant user expertise and circumstances, not a generic “everyone” audience (pp. 33, 36–37). Distinguish sponsors/proxies from people who do the work. The therapy-advisor example illustrates different learning constraints for roles; it does not establish that every doctor or technician behaves identically. The book suggests naming actual people for follow-up; design-pro uses consented/anonymized participant identifiers where appropriate rather than placing unnecessary identities in reusable notes.

The authors warn both against accepting every requested solution and against dismissing users' domain knowledge for a designer's favored innovation (p. 37). Their traffic users wanted to frame/evaluate alternatives before automated optimization. Treat a proposed future need as a hypothesis; do not reclassify the team's expectation as confirmed demand.

## Separate a task from a proposed scenario

A task describes a desired job; a scenario spells out actions and system responses for one particular design (pp. 40–45). Storyboards can make the proposed experience discussable. Whether repeated clicks should place another object or select the existing object depends on the actual sequence, frequency of repeated placement and need to adjust position (pp. 40–41), not an abstract rule that one interaction is always best.

Representative tasks remain examples, not exhaustive proof. Reconcile them with broader requirements and consider untested variations (pp. 41–45). The source's strict-waterfall discussion is historically framed; its useful distinction is integrated jobs versus isolated functions, not a claim about every current organization's process.

Recruitment difficulty should trigger investigation of access, relevance and incentives, but is not by itself proof that no market or need exists. The book's claims equating inability to recruit with inability to obtain users are too categorical (pp. 31–33, 97). Likewise its broad Europe/U.S. cultural, employment-law, productivity and motivation assertions are the authors' 1990s framing, not verified current facts (pp. 37–40). The participatory-design passage does usefully distinguish negotiated collaboration and improved work life from merely collecting feedback after decisions are imposed.

## Reuse the rationale as well as the visual pattern

The reuse chapter distinguishes adopting an existing platform framework, integrating existing application capability, and borrowing an interaction technique (pp. 47–56). Inspect what the actual users already understand, the integration boundary and the maintenance/runtime cost. The spreadsheet add-on example supports reuse when it fits the job, not an instruction to buy particular financial software or always embed a spreadsheet.

The tool-palette analysis makes fit concrete: repeated graphical operations may benefit from a persistent selected tool, whereas a one-off command may not (pp. 51–52). Show the active mode and a usable exit; an icon's shape is not automatically meaningful. The book's “cursor changes make palette modes OK” is insufficient for touch or nonvisual interaction. An accessible name/state and relevant input paths still need implementation and verification.

The reasons listed include movement, memory, problem-solving, attention, conventions and user diversity (pp. 53–54). Keep information used together available together; label actions with task vocabulary; show useful feedback and recovery. The historical statement that graphical icons cannot be translated for blind users describes a limitation of the portrayed implementation, not a reason to prohibit icons or assume images have no accessible alternatives. Likewise the book's assumptions about what menus versus palettes can do are period-specific, not universal technical constraints.

Novelty requires a meaningful unresolved need and evaluation of alternatives (pp. 56–57). Innovation-heavy case studies can underreport mundane reused foundations. Neither a celebrated old case nor an invented feature proves fit for a new product.

## Visual organization without rigid simplification

Clustering related controls with useful labels, proximity or whitespace can clarify search and conceptual organization (pp. 58–60). A separate bordered container is optional. Important controls deserve appropriate visibility, but the source's advice to hide/shrink rarely used controls needs qualification: rare safety/recovery functions may still be consequential and must remain usable. Frequency alone does not determine priority or target size.

Consistency should preserve transferable behavior, while distinct purposes such as a critical error and routine help can look different (p. 59). Supplement color with another meaningful cue. The source's cultural color assertions and “7 percent of all adults” prevalence are not verified for current/global use. Its gray-first/minimal-color prescription and one-or-two-type-style suggestion are design heuristics, not universal brand, accessibility or typography requirements. A visually sparse interface can still conceal the necessary job.

## Cognitive walkthrough: a predicted first-use path

Before a walkthrough, specify the intended users and knowledge, the task, a sufficiently detailed design/prototype and a known correct action sequence (pp. 61–74). Finding that sequence by exploring is preparation, not the walkthrough itself. For each action, examine the reason to want its effect, whether the control can be noticed, whether its meaning matches the goal, and whether the resulting feedback shows useful progress (pp. 68–71). This is an analysis of predicted behavior, **not a test with actual users** (p. 69).

The photocopier story exposes an unlabeled power button hidden on the back, no power-state feedback, and the assumption that a person knows the machine needs turning on (pp. 66–68). The repair may be eliminating the extra prerequisite, not merely adding a label. Continue analyzing later actions after recording a failure rather than letting one early failure obscure the rest of the design. Distinguish “assume repaired for analysis” from an actual implemented repair.

The Chooser example (pp. 62–65, 72–74) is an ASCII storyboard, not an actual current macOS interface. The original shows a menu whose printer route is labeled “Chooser”; printer icons in a left pane; an initially empty right pane; then printer names and background-printing options appearing only after selecting a printer type. That hidden prerequisite and the unrelated-looking menu route require knowledge the task description does not give users. The upper-left close box differs from the expected confirmation control in this historical environment. **Do not generalize this to requiring an OK button on every current settings view.** The transferable question is when a setting takes effect, how that is communicated, and whether it can be undone or dismissed appropriately.

The walkthrough's critiques rely on a situated user model and should be tested, not presented as observed error rates. Group hierarchy may affect willingness to report problems (p. 68), but equal seniority is not a required rule for all reviews.

## Action analysis and heuristics answer different questions

Formal action analysis expands goals/methods/selection rules into small physical and mental operations; the informal version lists meaningful actions and examines effort, prerequisites, learning and documentation (pp. 74–87). Use the cheapest analysis adequate to the decision. A frequently repeated high-volume operation can justify more detailed modeling; a tiny predicted saving does not repair a task mismatch, missing recovery or incomprehensible workflow.

The tables (pp. 76–79) provide historical average action times, including broad typist ranges and guessed system delay. The example's timing is a **prediction for a modeled skilled user**, not recorded performance, a contemporary-device benchmark or a deadline for all people. The source itself notes different analysts can choose different hierarchies/scanning paths and require observation to resolve them (pp. 78–82). Do not treat alternative keyboard/mouse access as automatically imposing a fixed decision penalty on every use (p. 80).

The camera story identifies exposure calculation, remembering conventions, focusing and film advance as parts of the job (pp. 84–86). The authors explicitly say they do not know that the historical designers performed this analysis. They also acknowledge that people may intentionally want manual control. The depth-of-field reminder on p. 85 conflicts with the earlier aperture explanation on p. 84; do not reproduce it as photography instruction. The design lesson is analyzing useful effort and appropriate automation, with a remaining reason for control.

The historical nine-heuristic checklist covers language, feedback, consistency, memory, exits, shortcuts and preventing/recovering errors (pp. 87–94). Evaluators work independently before consolidating issues; a single model adopting several perspectives is not several independent human experts. The quoted evaluator counts and percentages are historical study averages with explicit limits, not guaranteed coverage. The source qualifies “all” as heuristically identifiable problems and still calls for other methods (pp. 89–90).

The one-person printing critique raises unavailable printers and queue changes as questions, not proven actual behavior (pp. 91–93). Hiding/greying a disconnected printer is not universally correct; the example acknowledges configuration might still matter offline. Task-based methods can miss unselected workflows; general heuristic scans can miss contextual sequences. Combine them and record gaps; even the chapter's combined analysis misses issues discussed afterward (pp. 94–95).

## Usability testing, assistance and participant protection

Choose participants and tasks relevant to the intended use; proxies can expose problems but do not establish target-population results (pp. 97–100). A complete job includes transitions, not just individual controls. Match preparation/training to the real setting. Pilot the protocol and document assistance, limits and deviations (pp. 104–106, 113–116).

The ethics passage calls for voluntary informed participation, withdrawal without pressure, minimized identifiable records and disclosure of recording/access (pp. 97–99). These are useful research practices, not a complete statement of current legal/institutional requirements. **Reject its suggestion to invent an equipment failure to stop a distressed participant's session (p. 98).** Pause/stop respectfully and honestly. Also do not interpret the text's regulatory claim as current jurisdiction-independent legal guidance.

A paper mockup can expose misunderstandings but only represents supported paths (pp. 100–102). Record unexpected choices before helping or substituting a screen; disclose assistance. A drawing interaction may require working mechanics to answer its specific question. Wizard-of-Oz/preloaded responses can test an interaction but do not prove a working backend or response quality. Disclose simulations and do not infer production readiness from them.

The book initially recommends an evolutionary implementation over throwaway prototypes (p. 102), but later recounts rewriting its own rough prototype and warns about platform mismatch (pp. 125–127). Treat reuse as a tradeoff; choose fidelity/tooling for the uncertainty and inspect quality before promoting prototype code.

Process observations help locate difficulty; summary completion/time/error measures alone rarely explain it (pp. 102–108). Think-aloud commentary is not complete access to underlying causes. Use neutral prompts, avoid coaching and record any intervention; do not turn recollections or ratings into direct behavioral evidence. The cited strings/stockings studies are reported by this book, not independently replicated or fully checked here. Separate concurrent-verbalization results from an unprompted timing study when that distinction affects the claim (p. 108).

For incomplete tasks, **reject the suggestion to fill in arbitrary very large time/error values** (p. 114). Preserve the actual result, stopping condition, elapsed observation and assistance separately. Do not hide missingness or present invented measurements as observations. The p. 115 suggestion to narrow participant backgrounds can lower variability while reducing representativeness; choose the sample for the question rather than for cleaner-looking numbers. End-of-session recall is additional interpretation, not a substitute for the observed sequence (pp. 115–116).

## Statistical recipes require correction

The small-sample example correctly illustrates that a mean/median can hide large variation, but its approximate two-standard-error recipe is not a general 95% procedure (pp. 109–113). The original rendering on **PDF p. 112 / printed p. 92** puts the denominator outside the square root in the pooled-deviation formula and uses sample-size rather than degrees-of-freedom weights. This is not just an extraction artifact; the page image was inspected. Do not copy that expression into a metric tool.

**External verification, 2026-10-25:** NIST's [confidence limits for a mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm) use a t critical value with sample-size-dependent degrees of freedom and explain repeated-sampling coverage; a realized interval is not assigned a 95% probability of containing the fixed mean. NIST's [two-sample t-test](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda353.htm) gives pooled variance as `((N1−1)s1² + (N2−1)s2²)/(N1+N2−2)` and distinguishes paired/unpaired and equal/unequal-variance approaches. These primary references correct the narrow formula issue; they do not validate the book's testing protocol or make a small convenience sample representative. Choose an analysis for the actual design/assumptions rather than using a universal ±2SE shortcut.

## Historical tools, useful implementation constraints

The UIMS chapter covers toolkit versus visual environment, objects/messages, event-driven interaction, external resource settings, application communication and Motif/HyperCard/Windows/Visual Basic examples (pp. 117–134). These descriptions, sales figures, versions, survey percentages, availability and protocol predictions are historical. They are not a recommended current stack. Object-oriented inheritance is not compulsory UI architecture, and purchase of a toolkit is not a blanket legal clearance for everything built with it.

The useful questions are whether existing components support the needed behavior, how easy revision/localization is, whether the prototype can reproduce the target interaction, and what runtime/version/options it depends on (pp. 120–133). The HyperCard account exposes delimiter assumptions, runtime-version failures and the cost of fragmented fast-prototype code. The shared-code checklist asks about mutable support files, vendor responsibility and upgrade compatibility (pp. 125–129). Reuse must include these boundaries rather than promising that integration is free.

Assess logging/debugging, modularity, extension support, distribution/runtime needs, supported platform interaction and maintenance (pp. 130–133). The examples “every dialog needs OK,” “no more than seven menu items” and “all buttons 50 pixels high” illustrate configurable rules, not valid universal requirements. Do not adopt them. A desktop prototype of custom hardware cannot verify all real-device behavior (p. 121).

## Help must be findable and useful within the job

The extended-interface chapter includes manuals, contextual help, training and support (pp. 135–152). Match these to the users/task instead of making documentation compensate for an avoidable interface failure. The proposed manual separates task procedures from detailed command reference and a synonym-aware index, but explicitly allows a smaller product to omit unnecessary sections (pp. 137–143). A concise task overview, concrete example, actionable steps and recognizable feedback support both learning and later reference. Additional technical detail belongs where it helps its audience; the source's “users never care about internals” rhetoric is too sweeping (pp. 140–143).

The vocabulary problem is particularly relevant to concept queries (pp. 143–145). People looking for “overstrike” could not locate a feature indexed as “strikethru.” Support terms people actually use and useful related concepts; do not assume matching any synonym establishes equivalent behavior. The book's use of Copy/Move/Paste as interchangeable names is especially unsafe when those operations can differ. The quoted match rates, survey sizes and possible fourfold improvement are historical findings reported in this book, not independently checked or promised for design-pro.

The source's claims that screen help cannot be effectively browsed/indexed and should mostly fit a single line reflect period-specific systems and are not adopted as current constraints (pp. 145–147). The useful questions remain whether someone will look for help, can find the relevant answer and can recognize/apply it without losing their place. A brief contextual answer may link to detailed reference; neither a one-line limit nor putting everything into one screen follows from this.

The minimalist-training section distinguishes active investigation from a complete reference (pp. 147–150). Its suggested practice, feedback, revisiting skills, varying irrelevant surface details and reducing tutorial-navigation burden are candidate instructional techniques, not independently verified learning-effect guarantees here. Do not deliberately omit information needed for safe recovery, or infer that training makes a confusing workflow acceptable. Choose the amount of guidance for users' prior knowledge and the task.

Support should preserve the current issue across a handoff, ask for relevant information, make required diagnostics findable and feed recurring problems back into design (pp. 150–152). A role-play is a rehearsal, not a real support outcome. The source's license-number requirement and estimates of telephone support costs are product-specific examples, not mandatory collection rules or current benchmarks. A development/support rotation is one possible feedback channel, not a universal staffing requirement.

## Appendix L is a historical legal discussion

The appendix explicitly labels itself **“as of 1994”** and says its authors are not patent/copyright attorneys (pp. 153–160). It discusses trade secrets, trademarks, patents, copyright, idea/expression and interface-copying disputes, then proposes boundary categories and a legal-review strategy. Its fixed protection periods, examples, predictions about look-and-feel cases, categorical copying permissions/prohibitions and philosophical preference for patent protection are historical assertions/opinions. **None is adopted as current legal advice or a license clearance.** In particular, being in a company's prior work or a toolkit example is not treated here as automatic permission for reuse. No current-law review was undertaken; this skill review does not need to resolve a live legal question. Preserve source notices and use appropriately authorized components/assets in the demos.

## Management, change and the limits of confidence

Appendix M favors integrated responsibility for interface, functionality, implementation, documentation and evaluation (pp. 161–169). Its example makes Enter repeat the previous function key, with the current binding shown only by its absence from a list; repeated attempts therefore produce surprising different results (pp. 162–164). Documentation cannot repair that hidden state. The story says the organizer misrepresented the system to draw colleagues into a failing trial; retain it as an account, not a recommended method of obtaining participation.

The authors oppose dismissing usability as somebody else's job, but their staffing caricatures and claims about particular organizational structures are opinions, not hiring rules (pp. 161–165). Coordination does not require every role to have one manager. The appendix's rejection of quantitative targets and emphasis on instinct/pride conflict with earlier encouragement to set usability objectives (pp. 29, 166–167). In particular, **“users will feel the same way” as a proud designer is not evidence**. Use explicit task criteria, observed problems, uncertainty, cost and consequence when deciding whether further iteration is needed. Neither a fixed two-iteration minimum nor a week-or-two polish slot guarantees readiness.

The time-to-break-even discussion usefully makes delayed release a tradeoff involving expected acceptance and costs, rather than automatically assuming either faster or later is better (pp. 167–168). It supplies no actual forecast for these demos. The advice to quit an organization that resists usability is not carried into design-pro (p. 169).

Product updates add existing users' habits/dependencies to the design problem (p. 169). The WordStar example preserves familiar shortcuts while adding another interaction route. Consider migration and compatibility, but do not accumulate options indefinitely or preserve a harmful behavior solely because it is old. This is consistent with purposeful variants and the book's earlier task-specific reuse rationale.

## Exercises reinforce application rather than certify ability

The exercises cover physical interfaces, identifying where task-centered steps were used, interviews about cooking/backpacking, placing word-processor controls, borrowed color conventions, metaphor failures, a telephone-message walkthrough, knowledge requirements and independent heuristic consolidation (pp. 171–179). Their page limits encourage selecting significant findings rather than an undifferentiated issue dump. They are assignments, not evidence that their participants or outcomes exist.

Further exercises require thinking-aloud observations with fallback help, reflection on missed usability problems, context-specific tool evaluation, a small program, a mini-manual and help critique (pp. 180–183). No interviews, volunteer recruitment, external messages or studies were performed merely because these exercises request them. Suggested sample counts and novice/expert time differences are teaching parameters, not universal research sufficiency.

The final thermostat assignment supplies fictional constraints: heating-only, multiple zones, limited panel space, delayed physical response, scheduled temperatures and temporary overrides (pp. 184–187). It asks students to check the assumed tasks with people, consider coverage, walk through, test, revise and give a scenario-grounded rationale. Do not treat the supplied market survey or marketing preferences as real evidence, nor use its historical hardware restrictions in a Web/Mobile design. Its assignment calls the initial situated job descriptions “scenarios” even though chapter 2 distinguishes design-specific scenarios from solution-independent tasks; keep that distinction explicit in design-pro. The closing index links terms and synonyms (pp. 188–190), not additional research evidence.

## Application to both design-pro modes

For the Web game page, an end-to-end task could be identifying whether the game fits a person's interests/device, finding release information, and understanding what the primary action will do. For Mobile chat, start with finding an existing conversation, sending a message, recognizing pending/failure, recovering once and continuing without duplicated messages. These are design-pro demo hypotheses, not user research.

Keep a mode's operational instructions concise; retrieve these task, rationale and evaluation distinctions when relevant. A narrow spacing repair does not require the whole book's project lifecycle. Help and query routing should support understandable synonyms and relevant depth; appending an extensive manual to each answer would defeat that purpose.
