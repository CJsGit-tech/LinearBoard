# X15 — UX Design Process Best Practices

**Source:** *UX Design Process Best Practices*, UXPin, copyright 2015 (p. 3). Cover subtitle: *Documentation for Moving Design Forward*; title-page subtitle: *Documentation for Driving Design Forward* (pp. 1–2). Credits list Jerry Cao, Kamil Zieba, Matt Ellis and Ben Gremillion (p. 6). Original filename: `User Experience/UX Design Process Best Practices.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. Historical vendor examples overlap earlier process guides; they are not independent validation.

## Useful documentation, not an automatic artifact sequence

Documentation should improve decisions, communication and later retrieval (pp. 7–23). The research/design/testing overview is iterative; p. 10's “always one after another” wording conflicts with its adjacent instruction to test alongside design. Read the practical point as revisiting understanding and design when evidence changes, not a compulsory linear method or guaranteed convergence to perfection.

Stakeholder/user interviews, surveys, competitive review, personas, scenarios, journeys, sketches, maps, wireframes and prototypes each serve different questions (pp. 11–22). Choose relevant artifacts. Handoff, history and accountability can be useful purposes, so the book's suggestion that documentation written for handoff is probably wasted is too broad (p. 23). Do not dismiss a needed change record as merely a waterfall relic (p. 40). Conversely, producing every artifact is not proof of a better experience.

The illustrated sitemap maps pages; the flow illustration links screen/action transitions; paper and interactive examples show different representations (pp. 16–19). A screenshot cannot verify the illustrated interactions, current tooling or test-platform capability. The rainbow table on p. 22 recurs on p. 89.

## Requirements and discovery

Separate business/sponsor outcomes, user needs and technical behavior/quality constraints (pp. 24–30). Review existing requirements before questioning people. Look for assumptions, prescribed solutions and contradictions; ask follow-ups that expose the actual concern. A stakeholder's answer establishes their expectation, not every user's need. Record the origin and confidence of each important requirement.

Forty-five-to-sixty-minute interviews are a scheduling heuristic; recording is not mandatory or self-authorizing (p. 30). Useful notes can document an interview. Recording does not eliminate misinterpretation, and wider access needs appropriate authorization and participant handling. Ask only for relevant background, not demographic details by habit.

User interviews should explore actual behavior, circumstances, frustration and desired outcomes beyond likes/dislikes (pp. 31–34). Open questions and well-judged silence can add detail. User reports are neither gospel nor merely worthless opinions: compare them with other evidence and distinguish recalled behavior from observed action. Too many observers may change the situation. Natural context or casual rapport does not automatically remove bias.

Surveys are a different measurement format, not categorically inferior “interviews light” (pp. 35–37). A shorter survey can reduce burden but does not guarantee better data; five/eight questions or three minutes are not universal limits. Order and wording affect responses. Larger reach does not by itself establish representativeness. Competitive audits generate comparisons and hypotheses, not proof that every common feature is expected or effective (pp. 38–39). Five reviewers and a radar chart are optional; preserve criteria, scales and evidence rather than giving a subjective score an objective appearance.

## Personas, stories, scenarios and journeys

The persona chapter strongly recommends personas and claims they increase product success (pp. 42–46). That is not sufficiently established here and differs from [X11's situation/criteria emphasis](X11.md#specific-criteria-and-meaningful-measures). Use a persona when it makes supported patterns usable. Source each relevant attribute and label provisional ones; do not infer “lazy” character traits, invent a quotation, or require a photograph to legitimize research. Thirty interviews and five-to-ten spectrum scales describe UXPin practice, not sample-size requirements. Nominal preferences in the p. 45 chart should not be treated as continuous measured traits.

Stories connect a role, desired action and benefit; scenarios add circumstances and paths (pp. 46–50). The book's feature-shaped template must not preselect a technical solution. A narrative's exact seconds, clicks or emotions remain hypothetical unless observed. In the job-search comic, filtering by experience reduces a large result set and sorting helps a time-sensitive goal (p. 48); the picture is an explanatory scenario, not study evidence. Common scenarios do not cover every rare but consequential recovery/access path.

Journey maps can include before/during/after service, goals, expectations, actions, ratings, positive/negative experiences and proposed improvements (pp. 50–54). The p. 52 empty template repeats “Before service” headings and shows no actual observations. It is not proof that every project needs that structure or that emotions can be filled in by empathy alone. Mark research-backed findings separately from opportunities and invented scenarios. The Domino delivery-tracker story is historical; do not infer actual tracking accuracy or revenue impact from it.

## Representation and fidelity depend on the question

Sketching can expose alternative structure without polishing details; flows show behavior while sitemaps show content relationships (pp. 55–64). The auto-deposit outline/shorthand makes a frequency choice and next action visible (pp. 60–61, 107). It omits many real-world conditions, so it is an illustration, not a banking specification. Tree tests can examine findability within an information hierarchy; they do not validate an entire transactional flow. Card sorting is useful where grouping is uncertain, not a mandatory step before every sitemap. An exhaustive page diagram or clickable shell can be expensive, contrary to the claim it adds no work.

Paper prototypes can use a human operator to demonstrate transitions, with care not to coach participants (pp. 64–67). Interactive wireframes can reveal navigation and structure; static wireframes can still communicate useful decisions (pp. 67–70). Neither format is universally obsolete. Low fidelity should preserve the content/behavior necessary for the question, not merely make every object clickable.

Mockups communicate visual decisions; prototypes communicate executable interaction as well as appearance (pp. 70–78). Keep clear naming, useful version history, realistic content, appropriate type fallbacks and responsive conditions. Do not require a separate static mockup for every breakpoint where an inspected fluid prototype communicates more. A prototype does not automatically replace behavior/error/constraint documentation.

Avoid unrelated filler and distracting celebrity names when testing comprehension (pp. 74–76). Relevant sample content is acceptable when labeled; copying competitor material is not automatically necessary or authorized. Fewer clicks is not automatically less work, a limitation the book acknowledges alongside the three-click myth. The 75–80% fidelity ceiling and blanket “don't skimp on animation” claim are not adopted. Choose code versus a design tool for the environment, existing skills and risk (pp. 76–77, 108); neither is always faster or definitively proves production feasibility.

## Test questions and tasks must match the evidence

Define the question, choose the method, prepare tasks/participants, conduct research and report useful results (pp. 79–96). Iteration can reveal errors but does not guarantee a perfect product. A hypothesis helps expose an assumption; stating it is not sufficient to prevent confirmation bias. Keep the study focused enough to interpret, without requiring one question per study.

The four-category taxonomy groups scripted, decontextualized, natural and hybrid research (pp. 82–84). Its examples overlap: a first-click test can be heavily scripted, and an interview/survey is not automatically usability testing. Rating a feeling numerically does not make it an objective fact. Use the actual question, tasks and setting to describe the method, as in [X17](X17.md), rather than inheriting the category's claimed accuracy.

A concise research plan records background, goals, questions, method/reason, participants, schedule and script (pp. 84–86). One page and five-to-seven questions are heuristics. If the goal concerns conversion, choose evidence capable of estimating conversion effects rather than treating a small formative walkthrough as a production experiment. Moderated and unmoderated methods trade real-time probing against scheduling and moderator influence (pp. 87–89). Unmoderated is not automatically cheaper, unbiased or free from low-quality participation. Record assistance and observe workarounds; recruiting/recording requires separate applicable authorization.

Write tasks around a realistic goal without naming the intended UI route (pp. 90–91). “Search for a gift” may inadvertently cue the search box. Closed tasks can have defined success criteria; open tasks allow multiple routes. Either can yield qualitative and quantitative evidence depending on measurement. Do not assume all closed tasks are accurate or force both types into every test.

## Reports should preserve observation, consequence and uncertainty

Show what happened, under which task/version/conditions, why it matters, and what to change or investigate (pp. 91–96). In the rainbow table, colored cells associate issue rows with participants; the diagram does not establish severity or causal explanations (p. 89). Preserve counts, context and contrary observations. Multiple evaluators can reveal different issues, but do not remove bias by majority vote; the cited 30–43% figure is a historical claim without enough methods here for a forecast.

Prioritize issues proportionately. Do not blame a red button for poor conversion when task structure is unresolved (pp. 94–95). Distinguish the observed difficulty from a hypothesized root cause and the proposed remedy. A report can be one file with linked evidence; it need not be a universally accessible cloud folder or exclude every high-fidelity example. Share only relevant authorized participant material. Discussion and follow-up matter more than simply handing off a folder.

## Design sprints are a constrained learning option

The guide's five-day sequence covers analysis/goals, ideation, flows, prototype and user testing, with preparation in advance (pp. 97–110). Reuse existing research, define responsibilities and arrange appropriate participation. Five users, a one-week timeline, mandatory executive attendance and a separate design tool are contextual practices, not guarantees of substantial/representative data or efficiency. The text announces four reasons but lists three (p. 99); this is an editorial inconsistency, not a missing validated principle.

Silent idea generation/grouping and later discussion can diversify contributions; votes inform a decision rather than prove the best solution (pp. 102–105). The studio's teams, five sketches and 15/20/40-minute blocks are adaptable examples. Restricting later work to designers is one coordination model, not exclusion by job title. Do not follow the source's delegation suggestion as independent authorization to spawn agents. Label the prototype's limits, use relevant content, test priority uncertainty and record changes; time pressure should not erase serious known failures. Page 111 promotes the vendor.

## Application to design-pro

For discovery, use existing context, ask about consequential assumptions/contradictions, and connect business/user/technical constraints without a fixed survey. For both modes, choose a proportionate representation, map necessary paths/states and define observable acceptance. In the demo evaluation, keep unprompted task findings separate from assisted checks, modeled reviews and actual browser operation. These applications retain the practical method without importing its compulsory personas, rigid scheduling or vendor-specific workflow.
