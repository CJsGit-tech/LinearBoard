# U11 — Web UI Design for the Human Eye

**Source:** *Web UI Design for the Human Eye: Content Patterns & Typography*, UXPin, 2015. Jerry Cao, co-written by Kamil Zieba, Krzysztof Stryjewski and Matt Ellis (pp. 7–8). Exact filename: `User Interface/Web UI Design for the Human Eye.pdf`. Citations use PDF pages, normally matching visible folios. All text pp. 1–72 read; 67 relevant original pages inspected, including all pp. 8–72 and the image-only cover/title/back cover. Exact image pages are in the separate coverage ledger. No pictured website was runtime-tested.

## Develop meaningful content alongside the layout

The introduction treats content and visual design as equally important and explicitly does not require all content finalized before design begins (pp. 6–7). The content chapter permits filler for early layout exploration, then prefers relevant draft content, existing project content or material that exposes the real constraints (pp. 9–18). Its useful mechanism is discovering structure, titles, date ranges and text length before the layout becomes expensive to revise.

P. 13's original diagrams change from equal lorem-ipsum boxes to a product process and asymmetric content area. P. 14 shows a public-style page with an unreplaced placeholder. P. 16 contrasts filler, print copy and rough exhibition content: the last exposes a longer title, multi-day dates and actual opening hours. Those are illustrations of potential issues, not a measured frequency of rework or a controlled usability comparison.

Use plausible proto-content and edge cases without claiming they are real research or final copy. Reuse authorized existing content when it still serves the task; do not assume older profiles/articles never need changes. The suggestion to copy competitor text and rename it (pp. 17–18) is not needed for these demos: create original, clearly fictional content instead. Neither “any extra effort always improves the product” nor “words are the foundation of every interaction” is a universal result. Nonvisual access also matters; the opening claim that vision is everyone's strongest sense must not exclude people who use other modalities.

## F and Z are not compulsory routes through a page

The source describes an F-shaped scan for text-heavy layouts and a Z-shaped composition for more focused promotional content (pp. 19–45). It illustrates headers, important copy, sidebars, alternating sections and primary actions. The useful question is whether the intended audience can find the information and action for the task; a familiar letter shape does not guarantee that.

Reject the following as universal prescriptions: upper-left always noticed, users read exactly 20%, every 1,000px needs an awkward visual break, row endings are best for advertisements, any text-heavy site ignoring F causes difficulty, and a main CTA must occupy the fourth Z corner (pp. 20–29, 35–41). The Freespee 30% phone-conversation claim lacks the experiment detail needed here to attribute an effect to F-shaped layout (pp. 26–27). No conversion lift is promised. Brightest/largest content is not always noticed first, and a screenshot does not establish a person's fixations. The book asserts eye-tracking validation for Z without enough study detail to adopt that assertion as general evidence (p. 34).

### Primary research cross-check

Read on **2026-10-25**, distinct from this book:

- Jakob Nielsen's [original F-pattern article, 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/) reports 232 users viewing thousands of **pages**, not thousands of separate websites. It describes an approximate shape with variation, not a pixel-perfect template.
- Kara Pernice's [later clarification](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/) says F is one among multiple scanning patterns. It concerns assessment of the content area, rather than a rule about inspecting navigation. Sparse formatting can leave important material overlooked; clear headings, meaningful opening words, grouping and useful links can guide people to relevant content. Motivation, task and layout matter, and reading direction changes the pattern.

These are the publisher's research interpretations, not a standard or a new eye-tracking study of our demos. Their clarification is incompatible with treating F as an obligatory whole-page layout or a guaranteed advertising position. The Z narrative remains a composition hypothesis needing actual evaluation, not a verified universal alternative.

### Original scanning gallery

- Speckyboy has numbered editorial F arrows (p. 21); Inc. and New Yorker combine primary stories with secondary/promotional regions (pp. 23–24). The arrows are annotations, not gaze observations. iZettle overlays copy/action on a photograph (p. 25). Freespee combines a header phone number with hero copy/action (p. 26); Kickstarter changes section structure (p. 28).
- CoDrops/Abduzeedo, CreativeBloq/TED, Amazon/Entrepreneur and Cycle Love/Design Milk differ in article grids, image size, sidebars, search and listing structures (pp. 30–33). Shared rows do not establish the same reading behavior or require a sidebar.
- A simple four-point Z diagram (p. 34) and an annotated Mailchimp hero (p. 36) illustrate the suggested route. Marketo, Kissmetrics, Evernote, Dropbox and Helbak show differing heroes, alternating sections or product grids (pp. 35, 38–40). Their visual diversity undermines an exact template.
- Airbnb/Hulu, Dunckelfeld/Comedy Central, Mission Bicycle/Brit & Co and The Kings of Summer (pp. 42–45) use prominent imagery and varied actions. These pages sit in the Z chapter but retain erroneous F-pattern running headers. Treat that as an editorial inconsistency, not a reason to misclassify the examples.

## Typography supports meaning; it cannot replace it

The typography discussion separates literal wording, visual treatment, audience and message purpose (pp. 46–56). Consider their interaction, including whether a heading, error or long passage needs a different role. A type choice need not be literal or decorative to fit the intended tone. The STOP contrast is described in prose (pp. 48–49), but the expected pair of images is absent from these original pages; do not invent a viewed illustration. The persona p. 51 is an example representation, not evidence that an actual target user interpreted the type as intended.

The NYTimes logotype, TD Bank/home versus microsite and Teen Vogue demonstrate context and scale differences (pp. 52–55). The author's age/fashion/gender/status interpretations and claims that green produces trust/wealth/serenity are untested associations, not reliable audience facts. Imagining a persona does not substitute for observing representative people. Neither emotional tone nor visual appeal overrides precise wording of an important instruction/error.

Distinguish character recognition from readability of a passage and inspect the actual text, size, weight, spacing, language and background together (pp. 56–60). The Calligraffiti/Crimson comparison on p. 57 illustrates two particular samples, not proof all serif fonts are superior. The p. 58 instruction to use white whenever the background is gray is unsafe as a blanket rule: test the actual contrast. The p. 59 original image/prose first describes the smaller, fully justified right sample as less readable, then mistakenly calls that right example the model to follow. Preserve the visible comparison and flag the contradictory sentence. It changes multiple factors, so it cannot isolate an effect.

The source's 60–70 characters per line and line-height 1.3–1.5 are contextual Latin-text heuristics (pp. 59–60). Do not require them for CJK, every phone width or a short label, or equate them with WCAG conformance. Current text-spacing tolerance and actual reflow remain separate checks.

## Hierarchy by role rather than a fixed quota

Primary/secondary/body and occasional emphasis can explain title, metadata, text and action relationships (pp. 61–69). Size, weight, color, position, spacing and type contrast combine; no minimum of three levels is necessary for every small interface. The Jon Tangerine title/date/preview image p. 64 illustrates roles with different treatment, while the Columbia example p. 62 mixes hero, events and article content. Typography can remain expressive without rotating essential instructions or shrinking secondary information beyond readability.

The original UXPin examples show text controls and alternative treatments (pp. 65–68); Talk PR uses open space, the zombie-story title expressive orientation, and Potbelly a denser menu texture (pp. 68–69). They are contextual illustrations, not proof that warmth, tilt or texture produces attention/engagement. All-caps/italics claims concern particular scripts; apply the intended language's conventions. A functional text interface does not become unusable merely because it resembles a command prompt.

Pp. 70–72 supply resources and vendor trial/promotion. The index points its final resources/takeaway to p. 72, but body content is on pp. 70–71; citations here use actual PDF pages. Historic font/tool availability and trial terms were not revalidated or adopted as current recommendations.

## Application

For the fictional game page, establish actual title, gameplay description, release facts and primary action before sizing the hero; use expressive title typography without compromising details. For the chatbot, test long multilingual messages, explicit progress/errors and composer labels using the actual font fallback. These are design-pro applications, not tested outcomes from this source. Repeated examples across this library are not independent research evidence.
