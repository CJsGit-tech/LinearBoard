# M02 — Guide to Mobile UX Research

**Source:** Sneha Kanneganti, UserZoom, *A Guide to Mobile User Experience Research*. Research and references largely date to 2013 or earlier. Original filename: `Mobile Design/Guide to Mobile UX Research.pdf`. Citations use PDF page numbers; the contents page has extraction-order artifacts, so follow the actual PDF pages.

## Research selection

- **Study context, motivation and goals alongside task performance** (pp. 13–18). Smartphone and tablet settings differ and change over time; the book itself warns against generalizing device-use contexts on p. 14. Do not infer “on the go” from the word mobile alone.
- **Choose a method for the uncertainty.** Formative work investigates purpose, value, navigation and underlying assumptions with low-fidelity concepts (pp. 19–20). Task assessment observes behavior on functional flows, while validation compares against defined criteria and verification checks prior fixes (pp. 21–22). These categories help state the question; their terminology is not a universal sequence or a reason to delay useful iteration.
- **Distinguish what people say from what they do**, qualitative explanations from quantitative measurement, and the context in which observations were collected (p. 23). More than one method may be necessary to understand both context and performance (p. 17).
- **Choose remote versus in-person research for its tradeoffs** (pp. 24–27). Remote studies can reach participants in their setting; moderated work enables follow-up, while unmoderated work reduces scheduling overhead but depends on clear tasks and instrumentation. Remote participation does not automatically ensure natural behavior.
- **Keep mobile study burden proportional.** Short, understandable task instructions and limited unnecessary typing are useful (pp. 29–30). The book's study durations, incentive amounts and “speeder” thresholds are historical practices, not universal quality gates.

## Metrics and task design

The source lists effectiveness (success, errors, abandonment and timeout), efficiency (time and clicks), satisfaction, comments and interaction recordings on p. 28. In the retail case, completion was checked through answers to task-related questions and followed by behavior and overall-impression questions (pp. 32–35). Define success from the user's outcome before choosing a metric; click count alone does not show success. This last recommendation is design-pro synthesis.

The case's return-policy finding (p. 35) suggests testing a task beyond the principal purchase action. For a game release page, test finding platform/release details as well as its main action. For chat, test retrying a failed message as well as sending one. These examples are synthesis, not source case studies.

## Exceptions, contradictions and historical limits

- The book is vendor-authored and includes product promotion (pp. 25–28, 37–38). Claims of exclusivity, prices, tool capabilities and speed advantages are not verified current recommendations.
- Platform taxonomy and Flash discussion (pp. 7–11) reflect an earlier web. Do not adopt a required 18-column grid, “no scrolling,” disabling zoom, or identical native/web appearance as present-day requirements. Preserve accessible zoom and adapt interaction to actual platform needs; verify current requirements separately.
- Page 29 favors closed questions, but this is guidance for high-volume unmoderated studies, not a prohibition on open exploration during discovery.
- The fashion study is dated April 2013. Page 32 describes 200 users in each of three countries and 50 responses per brand, while p. 35 summarizes 200 responses in six days. The geographic/sample denominator is ambiguous in this document; do not publish a confident pooled sample or treat it as representative of all mobile users.
- Device, spending, conversion and return-rate statistics are period-specific (pp. 5–6, 11, 13). A prototype exercise or AI interview does not establish population-level usability.
