# X04 — Agile UX in the Enterprise

**Source:** UXPin, *Agile UX in the Enterprise: A Step-by-Step Survival Guide*, copyright 2015. Author biographies on p. 4 identify Germaine (product manager) and Jerry Cao; no surname is supplied in Germaine's extracted biography. Original filename: `User Experience/Agile UX in the Enterprise - A Step-by-Step Survival Guide.pdf`. All locators are PDF pages; body running titles also say *Finding the Way Forward*.

## Understand work before entering a delivery cycle

Bring UX into research and backlog decisions before late architectural or release-date surprises (pp. 5–6). Ask about users, existing tools, manual workarounds, needs and business constraints (pp. 7–10). The author's customer visits illustrate how direct observation and questions about actual devices informed a design; they do not establish that phone ownership alone predicts ability or preference.

Separate the solution's purpose, functional scope, platform and visual direction, while recognizing that these discussions influence each other (pp. 11–12). Include relevant expertise early, without converting critique into a committee vote (pp. 12–13). A menu or layout may be affected by actual content and discoverability needs; use realistic copy before high fidelity, rather than discovering after implementation that labels do not fit (pp. 13–15).

The SEO anecdote is about overlooked requirements, not a current search-ranking prescription. Its suggestion to insert competitor content is not authorization to copy or publish another product's material. Fictional demo copy should be original and identified as sample content; this is design-pro qualification.

## Adapt fidelity and coordination to the constraint

Use planning to explain why an interaction is needed and let developers identify feasibility concerns (pp. 16–17). An organization's transition to iterative work may require more visible up-front structure; the author's mid-fidelity wireframes included relevant color, states and clickable flows without the cost of polished mockups (pp. 18–20).

The adjacent Yelp low/high-fidelity examples show the same basic search and category structure with different visual refinement (pp. 21–22). They support choosing fidelity for an evaluation question, not always passing through particular vendor tools. The bare-bones case used simple controls to establish data and content behavior before later visual work (pp. 23–24). Do not mistake this anecdote for permission to postpone essential usability, access or error handling indefinitely.

Assign responsibility for implementation quality and technical/design feedback (pp. 24–26). The front-end “gatekeeper” is a proposed team arrangement, not a universal role or a reason to add a bottleneck. Actual responsibilities should fit the existing team. Descriptions of Scrum roles and meeting cadence are historical practitioner usage rather than a verified methodology specification.

## Verification methods are different

The source **explicitly distinguishes a designer's implementation walkthrough from usability/user testing** (p. 27). Alignment, typography and conformance checks can identify visual defects, but do not show that intended users can complete the task. Schedule these checks around relevant changes and risk rather than mechanically testing every build (p. 28).

Acceptance work follows core workflows and compares the result with the intended style guide, while investigating delays rather than assuming every delay is a visual-design flaw (pp. 29–30). The task-flow screenshot on p. 29 repeats the signup/loading/onboarding illustration also seen in [X01](X01.md#connected-minimal-documentation). It illustrates transitions; it is not fresh usability evidence. The source's “at least five users” recommendation (pp. 21, 29) is not a universal sufficiency threshold.

## Keep context current with evidence

After release, combine direct user feedback with support, sales and account-team observations (pp. 31–35). Workarounds with a product outside the expected market can reveal actual alternatives (pp. 32–33), even if the source calls them “fake competitors.” Do not dismiss those alternatives merely because their vendors target another market.

Support-ticket patterns can identify friction and unmet tasks, but ticket volume is affected by customer mix, severity and reporting opportunity. Account-team interpretations and direct observed behavior should remain distinguishable. These are design-pro evidence qualifications.

Update personas and journeys when needs or workflows change; define useful events before launching a feature if measurement is authorized (pp. 33–35). The example of recording every function in KISSMetrics is a historical vendor practice, not a requirement to add analytics or collect every interaction. For local demos, observed browser flows can answer implementation questions without a tracking service.

## Limits and application

This is a practitioner survival guide based on anecdotes, organizational constraints and cross-promoted UXPin examples. It ends by rejecting a rigid, process-heavy approach (p. 36). Its universal headings (“always prototype,” “all feedback is valuable”) need proportionate interpretation: choose the artifact and evidence for the uncertainty, and weigh feedback rather than treating all claims as equally reliable.

For design-pro, preserve context and rationale through implementation, recheck corrections against affected flows, and report visual review, behavioral tests and representative-user evaluation separately. These applications support the two demos and their honest evaluation; they do not require an enterprise backlog for every query.
