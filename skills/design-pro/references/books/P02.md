# P02 — The Guide to Interactive Wireframing

**Source:** UXPin, copyright 2015 (p. 3). Title page says *The Practical Guide to Interactive Wireframing* (p. 2); exact filename: `Wireframes, Mockups, Prototypes/The Guide to Interactive Wireframing.pdf`. Author profiles in original p. 6 order: Tom Green, Jerry Cao, Marek Bowers. PDF text pp. 1–79 read and all 79 original pages inspected, including tool tutorial screenshots and image-only closing promotion. Body folios match PDF pages; running headers pp. 40–54 say How to Design for Content with Wireframes despite the rapid-prototyping chapter heading. This note uses actual PDF locators.

The source combines process discussion, analogies, reported cases and historical vendor tutorials. Full reading does not mean its external links, GIFs, UXPin live previews, publishing/sharing features or claimed results were executed. None was. Ideas overlap the library's other wireframe/mockup/prototype material; repeated examples are not independent evidence.

## Choose an artifact for a question

Pp. 7–21 describe wireframes as a way to explore structure, discuss content and evolve toward an interactive representation. Keep a clear purpose and an explicit distinction from final visual design. The source acknowledges contextual workflows (pp. 15–18) but also claims the only alternative to a wireframe is directionless failure and urges never skipping the gap (pp. 13, 21). Reject that absolute: a narrow change, existing component or direct coded experiment may already make the relevant structure clear. Plan before overcommitting, without requiring an extra artifact at every task.

The blacked-out tile layout p. 8, hand-drawn page p. 9, Keynote Menu/Carousel/Box1–3 composition p. 12, UXPin low-fi page p. 14, Photoshop integration p. 15 and interactive chart editor p. 17 show different representations. An X/gray box can establish a region without establishing its meaning or usability. The sketch/grayscale/detailed Favorites list repeated pp. 18 and 26 illustrates visual detail increasing; the functionality/visual/IA/sketch pyramid pp. 19 and 57 is a conceptual model, not a fixed production hierarchy or measured effort distribution.

The source calls mockups mid-to-low fidelity on p. 9 while elsewhere giving them the visual-design role; define the intended dimensions instead of treating those names as an agreed standard. Paper, slides, graphics tools and code each have capabilities/limits that depend on the actual environment (pp. 16–20); a slide artifact is not necessarily dead-end or a specialized paid tool automatically comprehensive. Do not assume everyone knows the same software, that imports preserve every behavior, or that a blueprint removes all later judgment.

## Content context, meaningful labels and consistency

Pp. 22–30 distinguish broad content blocks from enough internal detail to discuss what they contain. The Lenox map/globe illustration and zoomed-out Google map (pp. 23–24) are analogies for detail/context, not verified cartographic history. A header/main-video/secondary-video block layout p. 24 develops into navigation plus a lead video and four secondary previews with text on p. 27. That is a revised content model, not merely a change of gray shade. Pp. 28–29 describe its primary/secondary hierarchy and the temptation to add unnecessary style.

Retain labels that answer what a region/action is and use a consistent visual vocabulary (pp. 25–30). Rough representative content can be enough, but do not universally postpone all actual words, images or typography: content length, language, label meaning, image meaning and reading may be the uncertainty under study. The source itself recommends real rough content on p. 13, then says exact content is unnecessary on p. 29. Resolve this by the question, not a rigid fidelity ladder. A decorative placeholder must not hide the actual message or masquerade as a tested label. Do not copy competitor content into a deliverable as if it were the project's own.

## Signifiers and actual behavior

Pp. 30–38 connect visible cues to light interactions. The chairs p. 31 and video seek control/time display p. 32 illustrate possible physical/learned cues; recognition is not universally instinctive. A Home label provides more meaning than an unlabeled rectangle, but interactivity still needs implementation. The Home plus tooltip example p. 33 contrasts editor configuration with browser preview. Pointer change and hover-only notes do not establish touch/keyboard access, and essential meaning should not depend on a tooltip. Distinguish a designer-facing annotation from actual product help.

The repeated clickable-wireframe editor p. 35 and questions about color change, dialogs and size (p. 36) encourage specifying consequences, not requiring all three effects. Keep project goals and decision responsibilities shared; a single person can cover several disciplines. Do not claim a project is doomed simply because roles are initially incomplete (pp. 35–36). Iteration can revisit a supposedly locked-in interaction when new evidence appears (pp. 37–38).

## What rapid prototype evidence can establish

Pp. 39–46 use an expedition-tent story and an anonymous healthcare-portal account to argue for testing beyond attractive renderings. The base-camp/climber photographs pp. 40–41 are contextual images, not documentation of the described tent testing. The source's launch chronology, survival counterfactuals and safety conclusions were not independently verified. The proposed tent questions are explicitly speculation (p. 44); do not label them actual research transcripts. The healthcare anecdote p. 45 reports improved feedback after linking screens; it does not establish regulatory compliance, safety or measured clinical usability. A group being shown the intended path is not automatically an unaided task test.

P. 19 claims low-fi yields the same usability data as high-fi; p. 43 more carefully acknowledges that rapid feedback differs from in-context testing. Keep the latter limit. The speculative ten-prototypes-versus-two comparison p. 46 is not a measured fivefold productivity gain. Multiple variants and stakeholder comments are not by themselves a valid A/B experiment. A clickable prototype is not automatically an MVP or a shippable product despite the repeated terminology (pp. 19, 42, 54). Set the question, use adequate content/interaction fidelity, observe the appropriate participant/runtime and report what actually happened.

## Trigger, action and end-to-end flow

Pp. 47–48 separate a trigger and its action from the journey before/after that event. Triggers include input and page load; effects can show/hide, navigate, enable or change a value. The p. 47 screenshot exposes a trigger menu over Yelp-like content; the p. 48 flow connects signup, loading and onboarding requirements. These illustrate state configuration and flow documentation, not a requirement to collect phone numbers or create a loading screen. Executive/analyst roles are examples, not proof that each persona has exactly one separate flow.

Design-pro application: for a chat turn, state what starts sending, what is retained, what pending means, how response/error arrives and what retry/navigation does. For a game-page action, distinguish navigation to release information from a local simulated wishlist state. Include system events, interruption and recovery when relevant. A list of click targets alone is not a flow contract.

## Tab tutorial and state consistency

Pp. 49–54 construct six tabs, one selected-tab group state per tab, one corresponding content group state, and two click actions to update both. Original screenshots pp. 50–52 show Wireframe/Mockup/Prototype/Present & Collaborate/Usability Test/Design Management sections and differing active/content states. The p. 53 menu visibly has two actions; p. 54 shows the Mockup content view. This establishes the intended correspondence, not successful runtime operation.

Retain the invariant that selected control and displayed panel refer to the same item. Do not require duplicating six whole groups in implementation if a simpler shared state provides that contract. The tutorial does not specify keyboard/focus behavior, accessible semantics, unexpected events or loading/errors; those remain necessary when relevant to a real deliverable. Six is an example count. Its polished vendor text and media also show that low-fidelity is not a uniform visual category. No account/trial was created or preview executed.

## Motion purpose and precise transitions

Pp. 55–66 use the ambiguous dropped-ball description to show why trigger arrows alone leave transition details unresolved. Original gesture diagrams p. 56 and paired placeholders p. 61 leave direction, replacement/overlap and timing unclear. The historical Material screenshot p. 59 concerns visual continuity; a quotation in a secondary book is not a current platform requirement. P. 62 describes external pinch/icon GIFs without embedding their playback in the PDF. Reading those descriptions is not watching the motion.

Specify trigger, start/end states, duration and relevant delay/easing (p. 63), while first asking whether motion is needed (p. 60). Reject universal claims that mobile motion is always preceded by user action, that all users see a one-pixel bump, that every transition must look physically natural, that motion costs no work, or that platform context can always be ignored (pp. 56–60). A system response, reduced-motion preference and a target platform can affect the design. Distinguish transport delay from animation duration instead of attributing all timing to network speed (p. 65). Use [current moving-content checks](../current-accessibility.md#moving-and-auto-updating-content) when implementing.

Pp. 63–66 configure a tap and a Move to action, position, easing and simulation/edit controls. The prose says move down *by* 200px while the original action is Move to and the panel specifies a Y position; absolute destination and relative displacement are distinct unless the starting coordinate makes them coincide. Do not turn this into a universal movement recipe or claim the source demonstrates a correct delta from any position. The 300ms visible example is a tool parameter, not a standard.

## Phone-animation tutorial and recovery

Pp. 67–76 show a historical phone frame, guides/background, a 70×70 blue box with 10px radius, rows of boxes, an expanding upper-right box and a lower-left reset control. P. 71 says duplicate three times, but its resulting row and following steps show three total boxes; inspect the intended result rather than blindly copying the count. The dimensions are canvas values, not current native hit targets or mobile spacing rules. A phone outline in an authoring tool is not a native app.

The original p. 74 explicitly acknowledges the reset control disappears into the same-colored background, then darkens it. That is a discoverability problem, not an acceptable product interaction to preserve. The boxes still lack meaningful action labels. For an actual app, supply an understandable close/back action, keep a recoverable route, define focus and repeated/interrupted transition behavior, and retain relevant content. A nested advanced-animation reset is one tool technique, not inherently the best architecture. The p. 75 reset picture does not prove any of those behaviors. P. 76's SMS/email/QR share dialog is historical product UI, not authorization to send a prototype or proof of current sharing/privacy capabilities.

Pp. 77–79 reiterate iteration/refinement and end in vendor promotion. Retain scope/budget sensitivity, not the unsupported claim that motion wireframing is always quick or clears every misconception. Both demos can use direct runnable local representations and companion state/rationale notes without historical tooling. Actual input, reading, navigation and recovery checks remain separate from this full source review.
