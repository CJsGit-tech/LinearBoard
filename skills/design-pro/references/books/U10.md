# U10 — Web UI Design Process: The Visual Power of Mockups

**Source:** UXPin, 2015. Jerry Cao, co-written with Matt Ellis and Kamil Zieba (pp. 5–6). Exact filename: `User Interface/Web UI Design Process - The Visual Power of Mockups.pdf`. References use PDF pages, matching visible folios. All text pp. 1–26 read; relevant originals inspected, including wireframe/mockup/prototype illustrations and the closing promotion. Exact image pages are in the separate coverage ledger.

## Select the representation by its purpose

The introduction explicitly describes a nonlinear process: focus on purpose rather than an automatic wireframe → mockup → prototype sequence (pp. 4–5). Wireframes expose content grouping, structure and basic interaction; mockups explore appearance; prototypes add enough interaction to investigate behavior (pp. 7–11). These are useful working distinctions, not universal naming rules. A static mockup can suggest a control without establishing that it works.

A prototype's fidelity can suit the uncertainty; a highly polished representation need not implement every production capability. The claim that a high-fidelity prototype should always be one step below the final product is too broad (pp. 10–11). Likewise, the Apple anecdote does not mandate hundreds of prototypes. Choose the smallest representation that resolves the actual question.

## Visual design and collaboration

Mockups can expose conflicts among color, hierarchy, content and typography and provide a shared object for discussion (pp. 9, 12–14). Comparing alternatives before expensive implementation can be useful, but neither a static image nor a designer's immersion proves users can complete the task. Polished visuals may attract stakeholder attention; no general improvement in investment or usability is measured here. Mockups are not invariably faster to revise than existing code with reusable tokens/components.

Discuss project needs and intended visual direction early; keep the concept coherent and annotate behavior that a static image cannot show (pp. 15–17). Competitor inspection can reveal hypotheses or conventions, not prove that a competitor succeeds with actual users. The book's absolute “don't skip wireframing” conflicts with its own contextual opening and concluding flexibility; retain structure/interaction reasoning rather than a compulsory separate artifact. “High contrast font types” in its simplicity advice is ambiguous and must not become advice to reduce accessible text contrast (p. 16).

## Compare process options without imposing either

One illustrated route proceeds from sketch/wireframe to detailed mockup and development; another adds interaction early, then combines visual treatment with the prototype (pp. 17–22). The latter can reveal interaction and technical constraints sooner, but need not require a specific vendor or every fidelity stage. A coded prototype is valid where appropriate. Conversely, a quick visual exploration need not become production code.

Preserve what is known about structure, states and content when moving between representations. A detailed mockup still leaves unanswered questions about keyboard use, error handling, responsiveness and persistence unless explicitly represented and checked. A tool import does not guarantee those behaviors. Usability testing can expose issues earlier; it cannot literally solve unknown problems before they exist or perfect a design (pp. 21–22).

## Present context and use explicit decision ownership

Explain the user problem, task, constraints and feedback being requested when presenting a mockup (pp. 23–24). This helps reviewers discuss relevant structure/behavior rather than only color preference. Visual documents are means to a design decision, not the final outcome.

The source repeatedly excludes stakeholders from having the final say to avoid “design by committee” or diminishing designer value (pp. 16, 24). Do not turn that into a rule that overrides the authorized product owner or suppresses dissent. Clarify decision ownership, retain consequential constraints and weigh observations/reasons; design authority is not established by a job title. Collaboration can also expose technical feasibility, rather than merely handing off a static image.

## Original visual observations and limits

- P. 8 shows a gray Yelp restaurant search/filter arrangement with categories, neighborhood, price and sort controls. P. 21 shows a different Yelp home/search view with numbered annotations and a comment. They are not a before/after controlled result, and the screenshots do not prove linked prototype behavior.
- P. 9 shows a polished subscription-page composition in a design tool; p. 11 shows a typography-oriented page labeled an interactive hi-fi prototype. Their labels are claims about the artifacts; no interactions were exercised.
- P. 12 places mobile chat, food, transport, task-list and music-like interfaces side by side. It illustrates varied visual contexts without proving a shared universal layout.
- P. 15's overlapping design-thinking loops connect understanding, observation, synthesis, ideation, prototyping/testing and implementation. The diagram supports iteration rather than a requirement to create six separate deliverables.
- P. 18 is a decorative historical HTML-code image, not a recommended implementation. P. 20 shows a mobile wireframe inside a tool; it does not verify native execution or phone keyboard behavior. P. 24 is a meeting photograph, not user-study evidence.
- Pp. 25–26 promote ebooks, importing PSD/Sketch artifacts and vendor prototyping capabilities. These historical product claims are not current recommendations or tested integrations.

## Application to both modes

For the game page, make the visual direction and content hierarchy reviewable while checking its actual primary action in the browser. For the mobile chatbot, prototype the composing/progress/error/retry flow rather than relying on polished message screenshots. These are design-pro applications, not tested outcomes from this book. Source citations belong in companion rationale, leaving product controls focused on the user's task.
