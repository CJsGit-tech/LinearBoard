# X17 — The UX Research Methodology Guidebook

**Source:** UserTesting, *The UX Research Methodology Guidebook*, copyright 2018 on p. 25; no individual author identified. Original filename: `User Experience/UX Research Methodologies.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. This is a vendor guide to methods and their use cases, not proof of current platform capabilities or a comprehensive research/statistics standard.

## Choose a method for the unanswered question

The introduction and taxonomy connect research to objectives, product maturity and practical constraints (pp. 3–5). Quantitative/qualitative and attitudinal/behavioral describe different dimensions. A survey can quantify attitudes; observing a usability task can yield both counts and qualitative findings. The source's what/why simplification does not make an interview causal proof or prevent behavioral observations from suggesting explanations. Attitudes need not be measured only before using a product.

The lifecycle diagram associates exploration with early development, evaluation with design and measurement with live use (p. 4). Treat it as a planning aid, not a rule prohibiting later exploration or earlier measurement. Combine methods when they address a real evidence gap; do not commission every method by default. Larger samples alone do not establish representativeness or reliable inference.

Location affects which context can be observed (p. 5). Remote work expands possible participation but can exclude people lacking equipment/connectivity. Being in a natural setting does not remove bias; instructions, cameras and observers still change behavior. Do not ask people to perform unsafe walking/driving actions while attending to a study. The location examples are not authorization to record participants or their surroundings.

## Usability, prototypes and comparison

- **Remote unmoderated (p. 6):** prepared tasks, completed independently, with later review. Good when instructions/prototype are sufficiently clear; missing real-time clarification is a tradeoff. Same-day completion is a vendor claim, not a service guarantee.
- **Remote moderated (p. 7):** observe and probe in real time. Record interventions and task assistance separately from unaided success. Helping someone bypass a broken prototype does not establish that the interface was discoverable.
- **Prototype testing (p. 8):** test the uncertain structure/interaction early. Explain unavailable behavior so participants can distinguish prototype limits from their own confusion. A static concept can reveal expectations; it cannot verify an unimplemented interaction. Prototypes may be code; early feedback does not guarantee a successful launch.
- **Competitor studies (p. 9):** use comparable tasks and examine observed performance before preference questions. Counterbalance presentation order where appropriate. Two experiences per participant is a fatigue heuristic, not a universal limit; counterbalancing reduces, rather than eliminates, order effects.
- **Benchmark studies (p. 10):** keep core tasks, measures, conditions and relevant participant characteristics comparable across versions/time. Define the baseline before redesign where possible. Think-aloud can alter task times. A larger sample must fit the desired precision and design; improvement over time alone does not establish the redesign as its cause.

## Change over time and information structure

Longitudinal studies revisit the same participants, while diaries gather situated entries in text/audio/images/video (pp. 11–12). Match the cadence to actual use, explain burden and track missing entries/dropout. The one-fifteen-minute-session-per-week/four-week example is not a standard. More compensation may affect retention and participation; report actual conditions. Diaries are self-reports, not a complete passive record of behavior.

Open card sorts let people group/name content; closed sorts assess placement within predefined categories (p. 13). Tree tests examine expected location using a text hierarchy without visual navigation (p. 14). These answer grouping/findability questions; neither establishes overall interface usability. The recommended third-party tools and recording are optional implementation examples, not dependencies for design-pro.

Preference tests reveal which alternative someone prefers and why, after relevant tasks (p. 15). Preference is not equivalent to accuracy, task success, access or retention. An attractive option can perform worse on another goal.

## Experiment terminology needs correction

Page 16 defines multivariate testing as testing three or more versions. That conflates number of variants with number of factors. **Multivariate testing varies multiple elements and examines their combinations**, rather than merely adding a third version. This distinction is supported by [Optimizely's primary glossary](https://www.optimizely.com/optimization-glossary/multivariate-testing), checked 2026-10-25. Only the definition/combinations distinction is adopted from that page; its wider marketing/statistics claims are not incorporated here.

A comparison can test a whole design treatment; attributing its result to one particular changed element requires a suitable design. Neither a simple A/B comparison nor a multivariate test automatically yields reliable results quickly. Define allocation, measurement, sample/precision needs and stopping/analysis rules appropriate to the question. These are design-pro methodological qualifications. The source's live-site-only restriction is too strong for experiments generally; do not mislabel a prototype preference walkthrough as a production conversion experiment. Qualitative follow-up supplies possible explanations, not proof of the mechanism.

## Across devices and with people

The source distinguishes **multichannel** comparison of the same tasks on separate channels/devices from **omnichannel** examination of one activity spanning touchpoints (pp. 17–18). Same people versus separate groups creates different learning/comparability tradeoffs. Record device order and why the person changes devices; a prescribed sequence does not establish their natural journey. Differences between device cohorts can reflect different people/tasks, not only screen design.

Focus groups expose shared discussion and reactions, not independent observations of individual task performance (p. 19). Group dynamics may suppress dissent. Surveys gather reported experience/attitudes and can mix response formats (p. 20); think-aloud/video can change the measurement and burden. A large count or statistically significant result alone does not establish population validity. Use recordings only when justified and authorized, with appropriate handling of participant information.

One-to-one interviews allow contextual probing with minimal unnecessary interruption (p. 21). The vendor box on that same page describes unsupervised recorded answers, contradicting the moderated interview description. Distinguish the actual method used. Do not report an asynchronous questionnaire as a live interview or interpret a prompted answer as spontaneous behavior.

## Synthesis and communication

Compare observations with hypotheses and prior findings, identify severity/recurrence, preserve unanswered questions, and determine affected decisions (pp. 22–23). Mixed methods can add perspective without proving what is “inside the user's head.” Findings can support, contradict or leave a hypothesis unresolved. Share positive as well as negative evidence, with context and limitations; selected quotes or clips are not incontestable proof. Team review should invite scrutiny rather than suppress disagreement, qualifying the rhetoric in [X11](X11.md#validation-must-permit-disagreement).

Reuse available research before duplicating it. A shared repository helps retrieval, but “all stakeholders have access” should not mean exposing every raw participant recording. The resource list and vendor contact information on pp. 24–25 are historical; the referenced books were not additionally reviewed merely because this guide lists them.

## Application

For `init`, identify the unresolved user/task question before suggesting research. For `query`, select a method whose evidence fits the claim and state its limits. For both demos, distinguish model/static review and browser operation from representative-user, native-device, assistive-technology, benchmark or production-experiment evidence.
