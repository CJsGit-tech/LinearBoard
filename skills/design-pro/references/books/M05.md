# M05 — Mobile Developer's Guide to the Galaxy

**Source:** *Mobile Developer's Guide to the Galaxy*, 17th edition, October 2017; published by the Open-Xchange team, editors Marco Tabor and Mladenka Vrdoljak. The source states a Creative Commons BY 2.5 license on PDF p. 2. Original filename: `Mobile Design/Mobile Developers Guide to the Galaxy.pdf`. All citations use PDF pages; main chapter folios in the reviewed opening use PDF page minus four. This is a multi-author collection; chapter authors are identified where relevant below.

## Platform and product framing

Robert Virkus's opening chapter argues for adapting to the platform's strengths rather than the lowest common denominator (pp. 8–12), considering the target region (pp. 9, 14–18), and choosing the technology for the audience and problem instead of a platform allegiance (p. 18). These decision principles do not establish that the 2017 market shares, vendors or technology catalog are current.

Native, web, hybrid and shared-code approaches have different integration and maintenance tradeoffs (pp. 11–12, 82–87). Cross-platform concepts/assets can be shared while UI constructs still adapt to each platform (p. 83). The author's closing claim that a cross-platform solution is always obvious for gaming (p. 87) overgeneralizes; assess the actual platform features and game requirements.

## Intent, situations and discovery

Andrej Balaz's “From Idea to Prototype” chapter (pp. 20–46) starts with the progress a customer wants, rather than remixing competitors' feature lists.

- **Understand alternatives beyond the product category.** A competing behavior, workaround or doing nothing may serve the same need (pp. 22–24). Do not assume the competitor is another app.
- **Separate desired progress from a proposed feature.** A job statement describes a struggle and a desired future situation without preselecting the solution (pp. 32–33). A job story captures the situation, expected effect and desired outcome (pp. 33–35).
- **Ask about actual behavior and circumstances.** Switch interviews reconstruct how a real change happened, using a timeline rather than only hypothetical preference questions (pp. 24–31). This is a research technique for relevant participants, not a demand that every design requester undergo a purchase interview.
- **Expose adoption frictions.** The push/pull/anxiety/habit diagram (pp. 25–27) is a lens for interpreting a change, not permission to manufacture anxiety or assert motives without evidence.
- **Sketch the journey beyond the screen.** Map surroundings, other people and interactions between touchpoints; someone carrying baggage may not have a hand available (p. 37).

**Design-pro application:** init can ask what triggers the main task, what the user currently does, what result would improve the situation, and which constraints or concerns change the design. Ask only the material unanswered question. The source does not prescribe a conversational command or a fixed interview length.

## Prototyping, style and testing

Match fidelity to the question. A functional or visually detailed prototype can communicate a journey; paper, HTML or another available tool can work (pp. 38–41). Inspect the intended device rather than assuming a desktop mockup determines mobile type or spacing (p. 40).

Visual consistency can reduce learning effort, but intentional style changes can serve usability. Follow the actual platform conventions for type, icons and layout, and keep branding from obstructing the task (pp. 42–43).

Test representative tasks early, avoid leading participants, identify the prototype's limits, discuss observations and iterate (pp. 43–46). Numbers such as 4–8 participants, one-hour sessions, 6–12 switch interviews and 90–120-day recency are author suggestions for particular studies, not mandatory sample sizes or proof of statistical generalizability.

## Platform implementation chapters

The Android chapter by Vikram Kriplaney, André Schmidt and Daniel Böhrs covers the 2017 ecosystem, Material Design, architecture, tools, tests, distribution and vendor variation (pp. 48–66). Reusable design implications are to handle capability differences, localized resources and different layouts (pp. 56–57), and to test on a relevant mix of devices rather than assuming one emulator covers all manufacturers (pp. 60–61).

Alex Repty's iOS chapter covers the 2017 platform, Xcode, languages, testing, distribution and learning resources (pp. 68–80). Prepare for a user denying access to a resource (p. 76). Design/layout and platform integration choices are consequential and should use the current environment and requirements rather than uncritically following the historical catalog (pp. 72, 80).

## Mobile web framing

Ruadhán O'Donoghue describes a native–web continuum rather than a universal winner (p. 90). Browser/device combinations vary in screen, density, inputs, sensors and network conditions (pp. 92–95). Use the actual audience and target context to prioritize testing; the 2016–2017 market-share charts are historical.

Progressive enhancement provides a usable base and adds supported capabilities (pp. 100–104). The same section discusses responsive, mobile-first, server-adapted and hybrid approaches. Its statement that responsive design cannot adapt content is overly narrow; do not turn that taxonomy into a requirement to buy device-detection services. The React Native descriptions (p. 105) should not be reused as precise current architecture documentation.

Set a performance budget for the project, inspect unnecessary assets and dependencies, and distinguish functional testing from performance under constrained conditions (pp. 112–124). The RAIL numbers (p. 114) are reported historical goals, not a guarantee or a substitute for measured contemporary requirements. Client-side analytics can omit users whose scripts fail or are blocked, so an apparently healthy observed sample may miss a poor experience (pp. 114–116).

Ask for a browser permission at the relevant moment rather than on arrival, allow guest paths where the task permits, use suitable input assistance and avoid disruptive interstitials (p. 119). Do not interpret “do not require pinch-to-zoom” as “disable zoom.” Device and remote-debugging checks address conditions that viewport simulation alone may not reproduce (pp. 120–127). Tool/service availability, prices, free tiers, PWA/AMP support and beacon claims in this chapter are historical; no service recommendation is made here.

## Enterprise tasks

Ian Thain and Davoc Bradley cover enterprise strategy, workflows, management and security (pp. 130–141). The design implications are to involve people who know the actual business process (p. 132), state each app's purpose and audience (p. 133), preserve familiar platform interactions (p. 134), and mobilize the important tasks rather than copying every desktop feature (pp. 135–136). Audio capture is an example alternative to typing, not a universal replacement for a form.

Data synchronization and access depend on user responsibility and confidentiality (p. 139). **Design-pro application:** distinguish device-local changes, synchronization and the user's permitted actions in the interface. Enterprise device-management controls, vendors and security checklists here are historical context, not a current security design or a reason every design request needs enterprise governance.

## Game experience and marketing

Oscar Clark's chapter (pp. 144–170) distinguishes the core mechanic, the context that gives it purpose and the player journey from discovery through learning, engagement and departure (pp. 146–158). Device constraints, interruptions and the way controls feel affect the experience (pp. 148–149). Do not transfer its author's phone-versus-tablet assumptions to all users.

Set accurate expectations in discovery, demonstrate the experience before imposing unnecessary setup, and let users learn through doing where practical (pp. 150–152). For the game-release demo, show what the player does and the intended experience rather than a page of unsupported praise. This is a design-pro application; no real game or market result is established by the demo.

The chapter frames monetization around expected player value, timing and evidence, while warning that manipulative pressure damages trust (pp. 157–166). It also distinguishes awareness from direct action in marketing (pp. 167–169). Treat its numerical spending, retention and conversion statistics as historical descriptions, not targets or guarantees. Play-time/spend correlation does not prove that making people play longer causes purchases. Avoid fabricated scarcity or coercive interruptions.

The measurement discussion recommends meaningful events, session context, funnels and checking the data's accuracy (pp. 153–155, 165). The proposed level-completion comparison using menu launches on p. 155 needs a task-appropriate denominator; define eligible starts and completions before calculating a completion rate. Interrupted uploads and incomplete events can bias observations (p. 153).

## Connected-device roles

Alex Jonsson and Aaron Ardiri's IoT chapter (pp. 172–180) distinguishes a mobile dashboard, remote control and gateway (p. 174), and notes scenario-specific needs such as offline operation, real-time interaction and large datasets (p. 175). **Design-pro application:** identify whether a displayed state is an observation, a requested command or a confirmed device result. Do not imply successful physical action from a local button animation alone. Protocol/framework catalogs and 2020 forecasts are historical; this source does not establish current device support.

## AI capabilities and user expectations

Robert Virkus's AI chapter (pp. 182–193) separates application needs and levels of tooling, with examples of recognition, suggestions and conversational interfaces. It contrasts online processing with on-device privacy/latency and resource tradeoffs (pp. 189–193). The listed frameworks, vendors, channel integrations, forecasts and capability examples are 2017 context. They do not justify implementing a live AI service or claiming a simulated chatbot provides real intelligence. Medical and autonomous-system examples on pp. 182 and 188 are secondary claims and are not clinical evidence or supported design-pro capabilities.

## Security, privacy and understandable controls

Dean Churchill and Neil Cook's chapter (pp. 196–212) discusses personal data, trust, network/device threats, permission minimization, testing and privacy. UI-relevant guidance includes giving useful error messages without exposing sensitive diagnostics (p. 197), explaining why personal data is collected (p. 199), and minimizing requested access (p. 208). Those considerations support contextual explanations and understandable recovery; they do not require a security-audit workflow for every interface query.

Treat its legal discussion, device permission timing, security vendor catalog and implementation recipes as historical. In particular, the language/obfuscation recommendations (pp. 202–206) are not a modern security architecture, and the claim that a wildcard certificate is the only remaining route to a MITM attack (p. 206) is too absolute to carry into a design skill. Verify any consequential current security or legal recommendation with suitable authoritative sources rather than applying this 2017 text.

## Accessibility and independent use

Sally Cain's chapter (pp. 214–236) frames accessibility around independent task completion. It discusses vision, hearing, motor and cognitive needs and the ways system settings and assistive technologies change interaction (pp. 214–220). Do not infer that every person in a category uses the same aid or setting.

Prefer suitable standard controls and expose custom controls through the platform accessibility APIs; support user settings, consistent navigation and programmatic operation (pp. 221–222, 225). Give controls descriptive names and expose meaningful state changes. Use readable contrast and more than color, sound or haptics alone for important information (pp. 222–223). Test with the actual target assistive technologies and include people with disabilities in usability work (p. 224).

These are durable principles, not evidence that a particular implementation is accessible automatically. The chapter's OS settings, gestures, named standards versions, legal discussion and comparative platform claims describe 2017. Do not adopt its “always test on a Google device” recommendation (p. 224) as permission to omit the audience's other devices. For current web requirements, use [separately verified accessibility references](../current-accessibility.md).

For game discovery, explain genuinely supported accessibility features so players can judge suitability (p. 236). Do not invent support or treat a screenshot or automated check as full conformance evidence.

## Test strategy and evidence limits

Julian Harty and Marc van't Veer describe testing throughout design, implementation and actual use rather than only before release (pp. 238–243). Choose a relevant sample of devices and conditions, explaining what a result on one device can and cannot establish (pp. 240–242). Prepare controllable data, stable UI identifiers and replaceable external dependencies so tests can reproduce failure as well as success (pp. 243–246).

Heuristics and tours can expose alternative flows, interruptions, localization, input and network issues, but they are explicitly fallible (pp. 247–248). Virtual devices are useful but can differ from physical devices in performance and interaction; physical-device testing remains distinct (pp. 249–252). Unit, service and UI checks cover different scopes (pp. 253–256), and a test suite should itself be assessed for usefulness before declaring a release complete (pp. 257–261).

**Design-pro application:** use controlled simulated failures for the chatbot retry scenario and separately report real browser, physical-device, assistive-technology and user-research coverage. Do not claim a static check proves a runtime flow. Historical services, test frameworks and distribution constraints are not current tool recommendations. A discussion of production feedback does not authorize deployment or external testing of a user's product.

## Analytics and measurement quality

Julian Harty's analytics chapter (pp. 264–276) asks what decision a measurement supports, how events map to useful reports and how the implementation affects privacy and resources (pp. 265–267). Distinguish UI interaction, application logic, crashes and platform behavior (pp. 267–269). Comparable cross-platform analysis needs compatible definitions and data, not merely two dashboards (pp. 270–271).

Test/calibrate metrics, inspect automatic fields, and account for sampling, reporting lag, time zones and missing uploads (pp. 271–275). Start with known event sequences before trusting aggregate numbers (p. 274). **Design-pro application:** a demo click count is not a real conversion rate; explain what action was measured and what downstream outcome was not observed. Third-party SDK catalogs and legal/provider terms in this chapter are dated, not verified current choices.

## User feedback

Julian Harty's feedback chapter (pp. 278–289) distinguishes public reviews, in-app channels and social feedback; the content of a review may conflict with its star rating or be distorted by translation or manipulation (pp. 279–285). Categorize actionable issues, connect fixes to updates and respond to the actual problem rather than a generic acknowledgment (pp. 282, 288).

Ask at an appropriate moment without obstructing the user's task. A yes/no answer may mean “stop asking,” rather than the intended interpretation (p. 286). Do not dismiss an unclear complaint solely because its spelling or grammar is poor.

The source proposes diverting negative feedback privately while prompting satisfied users toward public reviews (pp. 286–287). Do not adopt that selective-rating pattern in design-pro: it biases public evidence and does not establish genuine user satisfaction. Historical app-store policy discussion is not a current compliance guide. Automated emotional/sentiment inference (p. 285) is not proof of a user's state.

## Monetization and discoverability

Michel Shuqair's chapter (pp. 292–308) describes download payments, in-app purchases/subscriptions, advertising, sponsorship, indirect sales, components and app-platform subscriptions. The useful design implication is to consider the intended business model early because it changes task flows, payment expectations and content access (pp. 292–296). Revenue generation is not proof that a particular interruption or upsell improves usability.

Advertising placement, intrusiveness and opt-out affect the experience (pp. 297–300). Distribution and marketing are distinct; accurate store/page content and an understandable product still matter (pp. 304–308). **Design-pro application:** a game-release page should communicate the actual offer and next action; a fictional demo must not imply a real purchase, subscription, review or release.

All prices, fees, market shares, expected earnings, payment availability, marketplace policies and license interpretations in this section are historical and need independent checks before consequential use. Do not treat the source's broad data-anonymization or open-source-license statements as current legal advice, or add monetization to the demos without a task need. The epilogue and publisher/contributor biographies (pp. 310–325) supply provenance, not additional design evidence.

## Disagreements and historical limits

- The discovery chapter argues strongly against demographic personas (pp. 34–36), while S02 recommends personas in UX documentation. A useful synthesis retains evidenced context and motivation while keeping user characteristics that change accessibility, language or constraints. Neither a fictional persona nor a job story proves causation.
- The source excludes gift recipients from switch-interview value (p. 31). That is a narrow acquisition-research claim, not a reason to exclude recipients from usability or accessibility studies.
- Native superiority claims (p. 11), interface-builder preference (p. 72) and market generalizations should not become universal design-pro rules.
- SDK versions, compatibility claims, vendor services, APIs, fees, app-store review descriptions, framework recommendations and training catalogs in these chapters are dated 2017. This includes Android 8, iOS 11, Xcode 9, Swift 4, old support libraries and Google+ sign-in. Verify any relevant modern instruction independently before use.
- The wine, mattress and airport anecdotes illustrate the author's argument; they do not establish universal motivations or justify cultural assumptions about users.
