# U03 — Consistency in UI Design: Creativity Without Confusion

**Source:** Jerry Cao, co-written by Kamil Zieba, Krzysiek Stryjewski and Matt Ellis (p. 7), UXPin, copyright 2015 (p. 3). Original filename: `User Interface/Consistency UI Design Creativity Without Confusion.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. Credits on p. 5 use Krzysztof, while the signature uses Krzysiek. Page 38 is an image-only vendor advertisement.

## Consistency is predictable meaning, not uniform appearance

Familiar conventions can reduce relearning, but consistency alone does not make an interface usable, accessible or desirable (pp. 6–11). Preserve meaningful relationships between an action's appearance, label and result. A decorative surprise should not change what a core control does. The source explicitly distinguishes consistency from uniformity, despite later wording that overstates how every repeated component must look.

The 2015 iOS quotation asks about platform patterns, internal terminology/behavior and continuity with earlier versions (pp. 10–11). It is a historical statement, not a current native-platform checklist. Existing system controls and earlier product contracts are useful starting evidence; inspect the actual platform/product before making modern API or numerical claims.

## External conventions depend on the audience

External consistency concerns the knowledge people bring from other products, platforms or experiences (pp. 12–17). Familiar menu terms and volume controls illustrate transfer of expectations. Do not assume everyone has used Word, that a carousel is always suitable, or that every familiar icon is understood without a label. A slider can suit relative volume while still needing accessible operation and meaningful values. New patterns need a task-based benefit, not novelty alone.

The Avis example warns against reversing a familiar required-field marker so that an asterisk denotes optional data (pp. 15–17). The visible form screenshot on p. 16 says required fields have an asterisk; the earlier optional-marker behavior is described in the text/linked historical account, not demonstrated by that screenshot. Plain optional/required wording can be clearer than a dense field of unexplained symbols. Keep the visual explanation and programmatic state compatible with [current label/error guidance](../current-accessibility.md).

Reject the description of users as lazy. Skipping microcopy can reflect task focus, access conditions or a misleading convention, not a character flaw. The claimed millisecond cost is not measured here. Color choice alone does not fix a field's ambiguity.

## Audit repeated meaning and behavior

The Reddit discussion and checklist cover color, typography, language, visuals, location and interaction (pp. 18–23). A style guide can document roles and expected variants, rather than forcing a single button type everywhere. Historical examples such as a twelve-point headline or four-centimeter logo box are not new requirements. “Green always means Accept/red Reject” is a convention example, not universal semantics or an accessible encoding by itself.

Keep equivalent operations named consistently; allow different words where they describe different operations. Save, Submit, Verify and Review need meanings determined by the actual product, not interchangeable decoration. A primary and a destructive action can legitimately use different appearances. The source's assertion that two button styles necessarily confuse users is too broad and conflicts with its support for meaningful exceptions. Dragging on one page does not imply every other page must support it.

## Purposeful differences and historical cases

Use selective emphasis to distinguish important content or action, or change an unsuitable pattern when it harms the task (pp. 24–33). Repeated appearance is not inherently boring, and drawing attention is not proof of improvement. Preserve recognizable operation while varying the treatment for a reason.

- **US Airways (p. 27):** a small striped logo could resemble a menu icon; removing it from secondary screens illustrates a contextual exception to a branding rule. The described confusion is a historical report, not independently reproduced here.
- **Jawbone (pp. 28–30):** different product content and presentation share important navigation and purchase information. The copy's health benefits, current products and offers are not independently verified facts.
- **Miranda July (pp. 30–31):** a linear stove-top message sequence fits a particular playful narrative while keeping forward/back operation stable. The guide's claimed addictiveness and the inevitability that a scrolling version would be tedious are interpretations. Page 31's “refuses to be externally inconsistent” contradicts the surrounding argument and appears to be an editorial error. Essential meaning still needs accessible text/input alternatives; a photographed message does not establish access.
- **Dolce & Gabbana (p. 32):** Back to Shopping and Proceed with Order occupy opposite sides of the same horizontal area, with distinct link/button treatments. Separating competing actions may reduce accidental selection, but no error-rate comparison is provided. The source invokes Fitts's law for grouping without movement measurements; do not treat this one arrangement as experimentally proven or mandatory spacing.
- **Amazon (p. 33):** checkout replaces global navigation with progress. The author acknowledges both a focus explanation and possible increased difficulty leaving. Preserve a clear route to revise, return or cancel; do not copy exit obstruction because it might benefit conversion.

## Diagnose actual contradictions

Xfinity's similar-looking dropdown/tab navigation may suggest different behavior under similar cues; mixed category labels may also make the hierarchy harder to interpret (pp. 34–35). A different subdomain alone does not make the problem disappear. The prescription to make navigation either identical or drastically different is too binary; investigate the task, labels and behavior.

Adobe's icon grid uses a mixture of suite/product appearances (pp. 36–37). The author infers that icon style encodes product hierarchy, then suggests another style for standalone products. That inferred code needs evaluation: adding a third appearance may increase learning burden, while clearer grouping/labels might be sufficient. Screenshots reveal visual treatment, not actual mistaken selections or current company structure.

## Application to both modes

Inspect existing components and preserve deliberate variants. For chat, keep send/retry/navigation meanings stable across empty, pending and failed states. For a game page, expressive sections can vary while navigation, primary action and link behavior remain understandable. Document any consequential departure and test recognition, completion and recovery with the relevant inputs. These are design-pro applications, not claims that a historical example proves usability.
