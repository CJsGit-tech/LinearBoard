# X08 — Mobile Commerce UX Design Best Practices

**Source:** Mobify, *Mobile Commerce UX Design Best Practices*. Original filename: `User Experience/Mobile Commerce UX Design Best Practices.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages. No exact publication date or individual author is established. It references 2016 search behavior and a July 2016 carousel article (pp. 2, 42). This is a vendor's retail guide combining its experience with secondary research, not a current browser/accessibility specification.

## Communicate the offering and the operation

Make the product offering understandable and expose useful category entry points (p. 5). The homepage example uses labeled category icons; its cited 30–40% category coverage is a contextual research recommendation, not a universal page-layout quota. For the game demo, show what kind of game it is and what the visitor can do next; retail categories need not be added.

Describe an action's destination or result when “Next” would be ambiguous (p. 6). The checkout example does not establish that every button needs a long phrase. Show that an action registered, including pending and completed states, to reduce repeated submissions (p. 7). Its bouncing animation and jQuery class names are implementation examples, not requirements; feedback need not always move.

Explain the relevant benefit when requesting information or account creation (p. 8). Communicate actual costs and qualifying conditions early, including shipping if applicable (p. 10). The reported abandonment percentages are historical secondary claims, not forecasts for a new site.

The source emphasizes lock icons, trust seals and the word “secure” (p. 9). These are visual reassurance cues, **not evidence of security**. Do not invent certification, imply an unused provider has endorsed a service, or substitute a larger lock for actual protection. This qualification is design-pro judgment.

## Focus without trapping

A focused search presentation can give the keyboard and suggestions room (p. 12). Preserve a clear route back and any prior state. Checkout progress labels communicate the current step and remaining structure (p. 14); use a progress bar only when its meaning is accurate. Separating sign-in and registration can reduce ambiguity (p. 15), but tabs are one option, not a mandatory account pattern.

Guest checkout avoids unnecessary account commitment where identity is not required; optional sign-in can offer real benefits, and account creation may follow a purchase (p. 16). Do not import the source's account-discovery mechanism without considering the product's privacy and authentication behavior.

The prescription to remove all non-checkout navigation (p. 13) optimizes commercial conversion, not every user goal. Retain understandable exit/back/edit paths and information needed for the decision. Do not force a purchase to recover from an accidental entry. The blocking mini-cart proposal likewise aims to force a choice (p. 38); a modal is not automatically better than nonblocking confirmation. Match interruption to the task, preserve continued browsing, and test its actual effect.

## Input and recovery

Use the appropriate keyboard, support optional password visibility, prevent inappropriate autocorrection and offer useful suggestions (pp. 21–25). The keyboard gallery illustrates differing layouts; its fixed count and code samples are historical, not a verified HTML implementation reference. A visibility toggle needs an understandable state and must leave the user in control of disclosure.

Address lookup and device autofill are different sources of assistance (pp. 23–25). Keep a manually editable route and do not assume a suggestion covers all valid addresses or languages. Avoid introducing a third-party lookup service solely to demonstrate a form.

Inline validation can keep a detected error in context rather than forcing a search through a long form (p. 26). Design-pro qualification: choose timing that does not report an error while the user is still legitimately entering a value, distinguish field errors from service failures, and retain valid work. The source does not prove that congratulating every completed field improves every task.

Infer known data only when reliable and useful. The card-type example (p. 27) reduces a separate selection, but a recognized prefix/logo does not establish that a card is valid or a payment will succeed. Name/address defaults and shortened forms (p. 28) need clear correction; shipping recipient, payer and billing address may differ. No magic field count follows from the anecdote.

## Access, navigation and selection

The guide supports readable labels, screen-reader access, a reason for requested personal information and non-color state cues (p. 18). Its rules for labels always above, two visual characteristic changes and full-width fields are layout recommendations, not a complete accessibility test. The p. 19 blanket 4.5:1 statement conflates text with control/graphic identification. Use [current text contrast](../current-accessibility.md#contrast), [non-text contrast](../current-accessibility.md#non-text-contrast) and [use-of-color](../current-accessibility.md#use-of-color) criteria with their conditions and exceptions.

Fixed navigation saves repeated scrolling but consumes content space (p. 30). The old Safari 44px “deadzone” is not a current safe-area rule. Check the actual browser/device, viewport and keyboard rather than hard-coding that number.

Full-row activation can make a single row choice easier to select (p. 31). Avoid conflicting nested actions and implement correct semantics. Drawers expose deeper categories (p. 32), visible navigation exposes frequent ones (p. 33), and a hybrid can combine them (p. 34). The Lancôme screenshot illustrates this composition; “combined is best” is not a universal conclusion.

Category titles aid orientation (p. 35); breadcrumbs may still help a hierarchy despite the source's blanket rejection on mobile. Quick buy is conditional on users already having the necessary product information and few unresolved options (p. 36). The named payment services and branding are historical examples, not current integration recommendations.

Show useful category-specific filters, applied choices and a way to inspect additional values (p. 37). Ten visible options is a source heuristic, not a universal cutoff. Visual color/size selectors can reveal choices and unavailable stock (p. 40), but need labels, state and access beyond color alone. The claim that all dropdowns are wrong is too broad.

The accordion comparison favors a left-side +/x cue (p. 41); it gives no sufficient methods to prove universal icon recognition. Preserve the established product convention, name the disclosure and expose its expanded state. For carousels, provide other content routes, manual controls and position information (p. 42). Its 5–7-second auto-rotation suggestion and hover-only pause are not current conformance criteria. Read [moving and auto-updating content](../current-accessibility.md#moving-and-auto-updating-content) before adding automatic motion.

## Evidence and application limits

The source repeatedly equates reduced friction and more conversion with a better experience (pp. 2–3, 13, 38, 43–44). Some friction supports informed choice, review or safety. Its vendor case screenshots demonstrate presentation, not measured causality, runtime focus behavior or assistive-technology success. Many keyboard/navigation principles overlap [M06](M06.md) and [M08](M08.md); repeated advice is not independent evidence.

For both demos, use clear actions, accurate progress, relevant input assistance and recoverable failures. Do not add checkout, accounts, payment, notifications, trust seals or tracking simply because this guide covers them.
