# U08 — The Visual Storyteller’s Guide to Web UI Design

**Source:** *The Visual Storyteller’s Guide to Web UI Design*, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 1–3). Jerry Cao and Cameron Chapman sign the introduction (p. 8); credits also introduce Kamil Zieba (p. 6). Original filename: `User Interface/The Visual Storytellers Guide to Web UI Design.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. Full text read and relevant original diagrams/screenshots inspected. Pages 67–68 include vendor promotion, not required tooling.

## Start with the audience and what must be understood

The opening asks designers to understand people, goals and context before choosing text, imagery, animation or video (pp. 7–10). A persona, storyboard or journey map can organize what is known, but no particular artifact is mandatory for every request and none creates observed research. Narrative can branch or be entered in different places; do not assume a person will read every section in sequence.

The book's claim that image-free sites are ignored, that bandwidth is no longer a concern, or that every site advertises something is too broad (pp. 10, 21). Text may be the appropriate primary representation. Choose an image for its explanatory or contextual role rather than a requirement to make everything cinematic.

## Do not turn psychology rhetoric into guarantees

The picture-superiority discussion introduces dual coding and sensory/semantic explanations, then claims sixfold information, 10% versus 65% three-day recall, a ten-second judgment and 60,000-times-faster processing (pp. 11–17). The book does not provide enough underlying methods/results here to justify these as design constants. **None is adopted as an effect size, timing standard or business case.** Remembering something, understanding it, choosing an action and converting are also different outcomes.

The age, Alzheimer’s/mild-cognitive-impairment and child-audience claims do not establish accessibility for a product (pp. 12–13). No medical evidence review was conducted; do not infer cognitive capability or a clinical benefit from these pages. The Bob the Builder screenshot has colorful characters and navigation, but does not prove that overstimulation or spoken navigation suits every child. Picture-superiority claims likewise do not establish that “most people are visual learners” (p. 21).

Lists remain text; chunking can help without becoming a picture (pp. 21–22). That distinction does not make lists inferior to rich visuals for a specific task. Diagrams can also be ambiguous and photographs can be irrelevant. Test what people can understand/do rather than decorating text to claim a learning gain.

The source does acknowledge that a couple holding hands could evoke different emotions for different viewers (p. 18). Preserve that uncertainty throughout: red framing, a puppy, a storm or a mascot does not prescribe one response or guarantee a purchase.

## Image categories are prompts for meaning, not universal icon rules

The author groups imagery as iconic, symbolic and indexical (pp. 24–35): recognizable resemblance, learned/abstract symbolism and an indirect association. Treat this as the book's practical vocabulary rather than a rigorous classification of every image. A thermometer's indicated state and a photograph associated with sadness do not have the same kind of relationship to their meanings.

The assertions that charts are hard to misinterpret, pants/dress signs are self-evident, folders universally mean open, or a familiar gear needs no label are too categorical (pp. 24–32). A folder could represent a location or grouping; a checkmark could mean completed, selected or valid. A navigation/action icon needs an understandable purpose in context. Reject a hover label as the minimum sufficient explanation for every input method (p. 27). Important meaning must remain available on touch and to keyboard/nonvisual users.

The hamburger recognition percentages (80%/52%) are a historical cited sample, not a prediction for a current audience (p. 29). Traffic-sign generalizations are also not verified current road rules (p. 28). The nostalgia example that people in their thirties grew up in the 1980s is anchored to its publication period; do not reuse it for a current brief (p. 31).

Search/free association can generate candidate imagery and possible associations (pp. 33–35), but image-search results are not representative audience research. Check appropriate permissions for assets actually used. No request to contact publishers or download third-party media follows from these examples.

## Medium and visual style follow the job

Photos, illustration, diagrams and mascots have different strengths, costs and constraints (pp. 36–50). Neither custom photography nor illustration is always more effective/cheaper, and stock imagery is not automatically unsuitable. Keep the product's meaningful visual attributes coherent without treating age, gender or a presumed “sophisticated” audience as a reason to exclude other people from images (pp. 38, 46–49).

Freddie can accompany actual feedback, but the book's attribution of MailChimp success to its mascot and claim that emotions increase conversions are not established causal findings (pp. 40–41). A friendly character is optional; confirmation, failure and recovery must be understandable without it. A cheerful animation is not appropriate feedback for every state.

For video, the source advises muting initial audio, controlling load cost and offering a static fallback, while suggesting 10–30-second loops and HD as a preferred standard (pp. 42–45). Retain the resource/fallback questions, not fixed duration/resolution requirements. A high-resolution display does not require a video background. A poster can preserve atmosphere, but does not automatically replace information contained in meaningful video. Text/media alternatives and motion controls need purpose-specific consideration.

There is an internal conflict: p. 45 discourages autoplay sound while p. 48 praises Borel's autoplay music as non-annoying. The latter is the author's judgment, not verified user preference. Do not start background audio merely to make the game page atmospheric. **Current external guidance, verified 2026-10-25:** W3C explains that automatically playing page audio lasting more than three seconds needs pause/stop or volume control independent of system volume, and recommends user-initiated sound. See [audio control](../current-accessibility.md#audio-control).

**Current external guidance, verified 2026-10-25:** meaningful non-text information needs an equivalent-purpose text alternative, with criterion-specific cases; non-text controls need a purpose-describing name; purely decorative material should be ignorable by assistive technology. Media has additional criteria. Adding pictures does not by itself make a story accessible. See [text alternatives](../current-accessibility.md#non-text-content).

## Optional interaction and a larger journey

The final chapter (pp. 51–67) substantially repeats [U01](U01.md), including Slavery Footprint, Tesla, rewards, Squarespace, Humaan, Babel, Mario and journey-mapping examples. It is not independent supporting evidence. All pages were nevertheless read and relevant originals inspected in this copy.

The chapter explicitly says stories need not be interactive; a static image/sequence may suffice and a quick information task should not be delayed (pp. 51–52). Personal input can make information relevant, but the questionnaire/Tesla examples do not prove engagement or validate a calculated result (pp. 53–55). Risk–reward and cue–routine–reward are explanatory frames, not guarantees of useful habits. Foursquare/Swarm and Dropbox incentives, quantities and growth stories are historical and not required features for these demos (pp. 55–58). The promotional share-for-trial screenshot does not authorize external posts.

Squarespace aligns product image and promise; Humaan uses staff/grid reveals; Babel describes scroll-driven falling; Mario keeps the main history available while inviting hidden exploration (pp. 59–64). Five-to-seven-word headlines, a claim that animation is necessary to prevent overload, and assumptions about every gamer's preferences are not adopted. Static images cannot verify the animation or input access; the Babel passage explicitly says so. Keep necessary content usable without hover/decorative motion, and critical actions visible even when rare.

A beginning/middle/end can organize the part of a wider journey the product supports (pp. 65–67). The comparison-tool example distinguishes buying the tool from completing the person's ultimate computer choice. The blank journey template is a planning aid, not an observed journey. Do not force the same arc or an exhaustive map onto a narrow repair. Product narrative remains subordinate to the user's task.

## Application to Mobile and Web

For the game page, use coherent world/gameplay imagery with readable release/platform details and a clear next action; important information should survive missing media and optional effects. For chat, illustrated empty states or a mascot are optional. Message progress, errors and retry need explicit understandable feedback. These are design-pro proposals to test, not demonstrated results or claimed cognitive benefits.
