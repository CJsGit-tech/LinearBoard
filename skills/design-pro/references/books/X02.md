# X02 — 3 Common UX Mistakes Killing Good Design

**Source:** UXPin, copyright 2015; author credits identify Kamil (co-founder/head of product, @ziebak) and Jerry Cao (p. 3). Original filename: `User Experience/3 Common UX Mistakes Killing Good Design.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; the source's final advice is numbered “5” despite the three-mistake title (p. 17).

## Design for the user's task

Do not let personal taste substitute for user needs (pp. 5–8). Journeys, difficult interaction wireframes and task-based evaluation can ground a visual direction. The BBQ Cultures example coordinates cooking imagery, a grill-opening interaction and instructional content rather than leaving the whole page as spectacle (pp. 6–7). This is a composition example, not evidence that the same dark style, drag interaction or video suits another audience.

The source's assertions about masculinity, male confidence and a presumed outdoorsman audience are stereotypes, not reliable segmentation evidence (pp. 6–7). Do not carry them into a game audience brief. Likewise, a historical mobile-friendly tool result is not a complete usability, accessibility or cross-device verification.

## Connect content, structure and interaction

The UX umbrella illustration includes visual design, information architecture, interaction design, usability, research and content strategy (p. 9). It usefully warns against judging the full experience from an attractive screen (pp. 9–10). The accompanying description of UX as how design makes someone feel is incomplete; task outcomes and access also need evaluation. The claim that vision is everyone's strongest sense (p. 12) must not become an accessibility assumption.

Start with actual content and relationships, then test navigation and interactions; visual refinement may happen alongside that work (pp. 10–12). The p. 11 sitemap connects top-level About/Work/Blog/Contact branches, nested content and utility links. Connecting branches to prototypes can expose a flow, but “always start with a sitemap” is too rigid for a narrow component fix or a single-screen task. Use the artifact needed to answer the uncertainty.

## Request only useful information

Determine why each field is needed and weigh collection cost against the task (pp. 13–16). The long-form example includes address, fax and company data; the shorter variant retains name, phone, email and comments (p. 14). Neither field list is a universal template. The text explicitly says there is no magic field count (p. 16), and a phone number can be genuinely necessary in some contexts.

The reported signup/conversion gains and Expedia revenue anecdote are secondary historical claims without enough methods here to predict results for a new form (pp. 15–16). Test task completion and data quality in context. Do not describe users as lazy, as the source does on p. 13; unnecessary effort, uncertainty and disclosure concerns are better design questions.

## Orientation and limits

Design each entry point to explain location and a useful next step; provide sufficient separation around actions (p. 17). Do not equate simplicity with removing essential labels, feedback or content. Its invitations to use psychological tricks and broad color-psychology claims do not establish universal persuasion rules.

For the game page, use the game identity, gameplay and release/action content to choose the visual hierarchy. For discovery and the chatbot, ask only consequential missing questions and require only inputs needed for the current task. These are design-pro applications, not proof that shorter is always better. This title explicitly draws from other UXPin books; repeated advice and examples are not independent supporting studies.
