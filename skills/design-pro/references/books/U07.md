# U07 — The Building Blocks of Visual Hierarchy

**Source:** *The Building Blocks of Visual Hierarchy: Designing Visually Intuitive Web UI*, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 1–3). Credits introduce Jerry Cao, Ben Gremillion and Matt Ellis (p. 5). Original filename: `User Interface/The Building Blocks of Visual Hierarchy.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match where printed. The complete text was read; relevant original covers, diagrams, screenshots and closing promotional pages were inspected. Examples are static historical evidence, not tests of their original products.

## Priority uses several visual relationships

The guide organizes hierarchy around size, color, layout, spacing and style (pp. 6–7). It explicitly says there is no single correct hierarchy, and its ending recognizes that users introduce uncertainty (pp. 37–38). A designer's intended viewing path is a hypothesis, not proof of where every person looks, understands or acts. Commercial goals such as signup do not displace the person's actual task or justify hiding alternatives.

Relative scale can distinguish important content, but simply making a CTA huge does not guarantee more conversions (pp. 8–14). The Teye screenshot makes a central product larger than four surrounding icons; the source says it is interactive and needs no explanation (p. 9). The image alone cannot establish that people recognize clickability, discover icon expansion, or operate it with touch/keyboard. Size, meaningful label, usable activation area and feedback are separate concerns.

Huge's large multicolored H, smaller Nike Making copy and tiny corner controls show competing levels of emphasis (pp. 10–11). That can make content conspicuous while making navigation hard to find. Shrinking everything else is not a safe default when essential text or controls become unusable. The cited 50-site average heading/body sizes (18–29px / 12–14px) are a historical descriptive sample, **not current recommended sizes or accessibility thresholds**. Typeface, language, rendering, zoom and reading context still matter.

The portrait/photography example removes competing content; Medium's pull quote interrupts a text column; Knuckle Industries varies decorative/straight text, color and scale (pp. 12–14). Use those as alternatives for hierarchy, not a mandate for minimalism, decorative H1s or pull quotes on every page. Large repeated quotations can interrupt reading or duplicate content in an accessibility tree if implemented carelessly; actual markup/reading order would need inspection.

## Palette relationships do not prove hierarchy or readability

The color discussion includes a car/dust/sky composition, a color wheel, Ondo's magenta/orange portfolio and a KLM colored-tile grid (pp. 15–20). A relative accent can distinguish an item from its surroundings. The photographs and illustrated tiles also contain scale, position, contrast and content differences, so color alone is not established as the cause of attention.

Do not adopt a fixed hierarchy of black/red over cream/yellow, universal emotional properties, “triadic is safest,” or the claim that a given CTA color will perform better (pp. 15, 18–20). A wheel relationship depends on the color model and says nothing sufficient about actual text/control contrast. The three-step hue relationship and yellow/blue example are not a substitute for explicit values and role-specific checks. See [U02's palette correction and primary HSL reference](U02.md#distinguish-palette-relationships-from-usable-contrast) and [current accessibility guidance](../current-accessibility.md).

The KLM example labels two different tiles prominent for different combinations of size/color (pp. 19–20). That is a useful qualification of the later “single focal point” rule: a page can have several task-relevant priorities, and priority may change through the flow. Do not invent users' interest in prizes or business-class information merely because the source speculates about it.

## Layout patterns need the actual platform and task

The layout chapter describes fixed corner/border navigation, horizontal choices, grids and F/Z patterns (pp. 21–26). The Adoratorio screenshot has centered content plus sparse edge controls; Huncwot aligns several categories in a row, with a selected/hovered category shown in a circular accent (pp. 21–22). Neither static image verifies persistent scrolling behavior, hover alternatives, keyboard focus or small-screen adaptation.

The source applies “prime pixels” and cursor throwing to website corners (pp. 21–22). **Do not assume a page's corner is a physical screen boundary, or transfer mouse-edge behavior directly to a thumb, stylus or keyboard.** Browser window position, viewport, actual hit area and input method affect the interaction. A fixed item also must not obscure the task or current focus.

The Hick's-law summary is too broad to prescribe removing options or forcing a single focal point on every site (p. 23). Group and prioritize options according to the job; hiding useful choices can add search/recovery effort. A grid supports alignment, but a particular vendor tool or grid is optional (p. 24). A visually aligned layout does not by itself define a meaningful reading or keyboard order.

The Speckyboy screenshot overlays a numbered F-like route and the MailChimp example overlays a numbered Z-like route (pp. 25–26). These are illustrative paths, not reported gaze recordings for these screens. The earlier “hard-wired left-to-right” characterization is not an appropriate universal reading assumption (p. 9). Content, language/direction, familiarity, device and task affect scanning; do not position essential instructions or actions solely because a diagram predicts a gaze path. Check discoverability and actual completion.

## Spacing communicates grouping without fixed quotas

The In Pieces screenshot separates the central polygonal animal/associated controls, side navigation and bottom links; Nua Bikes places a bicycle among large open margins (pp. 27–29). Huncwot supplies an alternative arrangement; RelateIQ groups the invitation fields separately from account controls (pp. 29–31). These show that within-group and between-group separation can express relationships without a border around everything. They do not supply a universal pixel gap.

The “almost 20%” comprehension improvement is attributed through a secondary source without the underlying method here, so do not promise that effect (p. 28). The 15 attention-points method is an informal planning metaphor, not a human capacity measurement or maximum element count (p. 31). Neither more distance nor fewer elements guarantees greater attention. Too much separation can disconnect a label/action from its content or require unnecessary scrolling. Too little can erase grouping or crowd activation regions. Judge the rendered job and actual target areas separately.

White space need not be literally white, and an uncluttered screen need not be sparse. The source calls excess space “dull”; that aesthetic judgment is not itself a reason to add content. Use the amount/variation that supports reading, identification and action, including intentional density differences.

## Style should support content rather than compete with it

Le Mystère de Grimouville's textured title/atmosphere, Jib Strategic's illustrative panels, Risotteria Melotti's food photography, 96 Elephants' image/text split and Kaspersky's bright statistic block illustrate different ways to distribute emphasis (pp. 32–36). The Kaspersky percentage is visible marketing content, not a verified security result; the author's guess that the background is a worked-over stock photograph is also not established by the image. The source's wood-grain description of Jib is not something to infer confidently from the small reproduced screenshot.

The source usefully asks that decoration preserve discernible letters and readable text (p. 35). A textured background may require a clear text surface or a different crop; an attractive screenshot does not prove contrast for every responsive crop or state. Photography, texture and skeuomorphism are aesthetic options, not guaranteed elegance or inevitable tackiness. Detailed imagery can help a game communicate its identity without becoming the only place that release details/actions are understandable. Vendor promotion on pp. 38–39 is not a required tool choice.

## Apply and evaluate in both modes

For the game page, distinguish game identity, gameplay evidence, release/platform information and the main next action, then check whether the composition supports that job at narrow/wide widths. For Mobile chat, let conversation content, the composer and recovery controls have different emphasis while keeping each usable. Do not enlarge a logo while shrinking essential send/retry controls or status text. These are design-pro applications, not user-tested outcomes.

Evaluate visual priority, comprehension, reading order, focus, target area and recovery as related but separate questions. The book's five visual building blocks can generate alternatives; they do not certify conversion, accessibility or intuition.
