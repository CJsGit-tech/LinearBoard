# X14 — UX Design 2015 & 2016

**Source:** *UX Design 2015 & 2016: Successful Trends for Digital Products*, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 1–3). Credits list Jerry Cao, Sergio Nouvel, Kamil Zieba and Matt Ellis (p. 7). Original filename: `User Experience/UX Design 2015-2016.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. Pages 1–2 contain the covers. These are historical trends and examples, not current platform requirements.

## Service, interface and collaboration

The first chapter (pp. 8–33) substantially duplicates [X13](X13.md), including iTunes, Vine, VW, Virgin America and Pelican Books. It is not independent corroboration. Read it as a task/service argument: interface choices should help a person's goal, with content, design and implementation coordinated. Preserve X13's qualifications: UI includes nonvisual interaction; five users and 0.1 seconds are heuristics; prototypes do not guarantee success; false scarcity and invented social proof are unacceptable. The seven-second Vine claim, search-ranking claims, mandatory-website rhetoric and predictions about AI are not adopted as verified facts.

## Consistent, continuous and complementary experiences

Preserve recognizable purpose, language and important content across devices while adapting presentation and controls (pp. 34–42). Screen size does not establish whether a person is at home, moving, using a mouse or needing a complete task. Historical survey percentages and predictions that m-dot sites would disappear by 2016 are not current evidence. Removing a mobile subdomain does not by itself guarantee a search-ranking boost. The book's claim that every separate site necessarily doubles work is too strong.

Change.org, Guardian, Smashing Magazine and Hulu show layouts with different density and navigation treatment (pp. 40–42, 53–64). Lookout replaces a desktop animation with a cropped image (pp. 60–61). These support adapting hierarchy and assets, not automatically withholding browsing/topics on phones, filling every whitespace, or requiring cards. Keep important tasks reachable when their presentation changes. A hidden menu has a discovery cost, acknowledged in the Guardian discussion (p. 54).

The source contrasts fluid responsive layouts with selected adaptive layouts/dynamic serving (pp. 42–48). Those implementation dimensions are not interchangeable. Use content failures and interaction needs to choose breakpoints rather than copying its six widths or mandatory twelve-column grid. The p. 47 benchmark compares two different WordPress implementations with different payloads and object counts; it cannot isolate responsiveness as the cause of a speed difference. Its byte/kb labeling is also insufficiently clear for reuse. Neither architecture guarantees faster delivery.

Mobile-first can help prioritize content, but is not universally the hardest/first problem or synonymous with progressive enhancement (pp. 48–53). Inventory content, establish hierarchy, test intermediate widths and adapt graphics without hiding essential meaning. SVG suits some scalable graphics, not every image. Do not assume touch devices lack all hover capability or large screens are mouse-only; essential functions need appropriate input alternatives. The 44×44-point Apple statement is explicitly historical (p. 52), not a CSS-pixel requirement or independently verified current native guidance. Use [current accessibility guidance](../current-accessibility.md) for web requirements.

Native versus mobile web depends on the actual capability, distribution and use context; the Hulu discussion overgeneralizes native speed, permissions and the needs of restaurants/small businesses (pp. 62–63). Do not choose implementation solely from industry category.

**Continuous** experiences preserve a task across devices, such as a draft, or connect an online choice to an offline action (pp. 64–66). **Complementary** experiences use devices together with different roles, such as a controller and display (pp. 66–67). Specify state ownership, transfer, failure and return behavior when relevant; these are design-pro implementation considerations. Screenshots of Gmail/Facebook do not prove synchronization works under interruption. Easier transitions do not guarantee loyalty (pp. 68–69).

## Personalization must have a useful, correctable purpose

The guide distinguishes system-adapted personalization from user-chosen customization (pp. 70–75). It favors the former's perceived magic; design-pro does not treat explicit user control as an inferior experience. A suggestion can be helpful without concealing how it works. Related content, recommendations, social connections, notifications and service-specific inputs vary in sensitivity and usefulness (pp. 78–80). This list is not a universal safety ranking.

Begin with the benefit and use context, then identify necessary evidence and evaluate whether adaptation actually improves the task (pp. 75–87). The airline example skips interviews and segments by gender/role/stress: treat this as a hypothetical, not established psychology. Page views, purchases, ratings and location can all misrepresent intent. The source itself later acknowledges that purchases need not reflect personal preferences, qualifying its earlier “nearly foolproof” claim (pp. 85–86). More data or use does not guarantee learning accuracy. Do not collect every available signal because it is technically obtainable.

Open interviews, focused surveys, scenarios and journeys can inform personalization (pp. 80–84). Under-one-hour interviews, five-to-eight questions and always placing the most important question first are heuristics. User research is not “indisputable data”; sampling, framing and interpretation still matter. Prior participation does not mean consent to unrelated tracking or increasingly intimate assumptions.

Netflix's explanation of a recommendation illustrates useful provenance; Spotify running, Facebook profile differences and Delectable illustrate different contexts (pp. 87–93). They do not demonstrate accuracy, clinical suitability, a current feature, or a guaranteed emotional bond. The 75% Netflix figure and vendor comparisons are historical claims. For a chat demo, do not imply actual personal learning when replies are simulated.

## Microinteractions are meaningful state changes

Small interactions can acknowledge an action, change a setting, perform a focused task or prevent an error (pp. 94–101). Specify trigger, rules, feedback, and repeated-use/mode behavior. The source credits Dan Saffer; the elevator/browser examples illustrate the model, although some descriptions of loops/modes blur recurrence with visual styling. Lighting a button or showing loading means accepted/in progress, not necessarily completed successfully. Preserve that distinction in chat sending/responding/retry states.

Apple/Google comparisons quote 2015 guidelines (pp. 102–104); they are not current evidence that every component should animate. Motion is optional and must serve comprehension without obstructing access. Use existing controls for feedback, keep a coherent tone, consider the hundredth use, and avoid turning a minor action into a long interruption (pp. 104–106). A 0.1-second acknowledgment is a heuristic, not a completion guarantee.

The eleven cases (pp. 106–114) include translation, Tinder swipe, Messenger's growing Like, Draft's optional editing constraint, Path hover, Reddit themes, Letterboxd CAPTCHA, iOS clock, Geeklist password feedback, profile choices and IMDb's rating joke. Treat each as a historical example, not a recommended dependency or live capability. Provide alternatives to gestures/hover. Translation need not be accurate merely because layout survives; a password-cracking time estimate can imply false precision; a themed CAPTCHA can create access barriers; humor must not obscure ratings or consequences. Optional creative constraints do not justify removing correction controls from ordinary messaging. Addictive use and extra time spent are not themselves successful UX (p. 115).

## Gamification should support the user's own goal

The trigger/action/reward/investment diagrams are conceptual models, not a validated law of every habit (pp. 116–124). Do not guarantee success from the 40% habitual-action claim or treat compulsive use as the objective. Arbitrary points/badges, noisy virtual economies and patronizing praise can distract from an already meaningful task. Let people set relevant goals and see honest progress; discovery must not hide essential features. Stopping, declining competition and leaving should remain possible. This qualifies the source's instruction to make people lose track of time.

Duolingo, Sublime Text, GitHub, Trello, Kiip, Nike+ and Reddit illustrate different mechanisms (pp. 125–134): practice progress, increasing skill, visible work, intermediate task states, external rewards, self-defined goals and social reputation. They are not equivalent forms of measurement. Contributions/stars do not prove developer quality; votes do not prove content truth; levels do not demonstrate language fluency; activity does not prove health benefit. Kiip's external rewards also complicate the chapter's earlier dismissal of extrinsic rewards. Whether rewards help depends on context and evidence, rather than “all rewards harm” or “real rewards are always meaningful.” Product counts, rankings, privileges and market-share claims remain historical/unverified.

## Empowerment, recovery and onboarding

The final chapter connects a useful goal, comprehensible paths, clear labels, proportionate interface, forgiveness and consistency (pp. 135–148). “Invisible UI” is reduced distraction, not invisible controls or an obligation to minimize every screen. Keep consequential warnings noticeable; polite recovery/undo and flexible input can reduce frustration (pp. 139–141). Disabled actions need an understandable reason where users could be confused. Natural-language date input requires confirmation of the interpreted date rather than silent assumptions.

The rhetoric that aesthetics improves motor skills, every delightful interface works better, or people enter a trance is not established by this guide (pp. 141–146). Credibility/ATM percentages and a ten-second first impression are not universal thresholds. Visual quality should be evaluated alongside real task completion and honest content. Tone can be warm without jokes or celebration on every action (pp. 147–148).

Onboarding can explain, let people try, collect necessary setup information or combine these approaches (pp. 148–154). None is always safe or superior. Teach the first meaningful task, reveal help when relevant, and avoid forcing unnecessary account/profile data. Progress indicators should describe actual steps, not pressure people to complete optional data. Images need usable text alternatives; 80/20 is a prioritization heuristic, not measured coverage. Medium, Carbonmade, Watch, Prime, Feedly and Tile are historical illustrations (pp. 154–162), not proof of current service terms, storage limits, speed or emotional outcomes. Pages 163–164 conclude and promote the vendor.

## Reusable application

For both modes, test recognizable tasks across supported layouts and interruptions. For the mobile chatbot, preserve draft/message state, provide explicit progress and recoverable failure, and keep any starter help concise. For the Web game page, prioritize actual game/release content, an honest next action and assets that adapt without losing meaning. Do not add tracking, native code, streaks or a gamification system merely because a historical example used one.
