## Purpose and mobile context

Pp. 9–21 advocate interactive exploration for communication, feasibility, early feedback and priorities. Treat these as possible benefits rather than guarantees: a prototype does not expose every usability flaw, shatter all communication barriers, produce feedback for all senses or necessarily cost less than the next implementation step. P. 11's 50% estimate accuracy, 80% clarification reduction and 25% post-release bug reduction are secondary case claims with no evaluated protocol here; they are not expected effects for either demo. Presentation approval is distinct from task evidence. A meeting need not always have a prototype (p. 13).

The recurring Yelp exercise pp. 15, 25–28 is acknowledged to be incomplete, with designer comments on unimplemented features; it substantially overlaps [P03](P03.md) and [P05](P05.md). A pictured selected Food state and successful home→results transition do not establish coverage, live search or usability. Longer time on site (p. 26) is not automatically task success.

The desktop/mobile comparison pp. 18–20 is a historical simplification. Mobile is not always on the go, desktop not always stable/seated, gestures not absent from desktop hardware, and animations not universally necessary or fun. Consider actual environment, input, interruptions, viewport and platform; do not infer capabilities from width alone. Mandatory mobile-only prototyping and the source's app-price averages are not requirements or current cost estimates. A mobile-web experiment needs browser verification, while native behavior requires its target runtime. Tool video/face-capture screenshots (pp. 16, 69) do not prove every thought or event can be captured, and do not authorize recording people.

## Independently choose the prototype dimensions

Pp. 22–36 permit combining wireframes, mockups and code. The strongest reusable distinction is p. 31's **representation, precision, interactivity and evolution**: medium, relevant detail, operable behavior and whether the artifact is disposable or a maintained foundation. Pp. 32–35 show that visual realism and functional coverage can differ. A visually detailed simulation can omit important behavior; a plain coded interaction can exercise real conditions. No fidelity name proves completeness, product feasibility or release readiness.

The source says rapid artifacts are not meant to evolve into full solutions (p. 29), yet allows evolution and public MVP use elsewhere (pp. 31, 35). State the intended lifecycle explicitly. Scope by the uncertainty and outcome, not compulsory horizontal-then-vertical expansion or endless polish (pp. 29–30). The named selfie persona is illustrative and stereotyped, not research. Reuse existing assets for a bounded change where suitable; new work also need not always start at the lowest fidelity. Lower documentation volume does not inherently improve collaboration, and distributed teams are not categorically incompatible with iterative research (pp. 24–28). Keep consequential conditions/decisions that a prototype cannot express.

## Traditional and simulated methods

Pp. 37–47 compare paper and Wizard of Oz. Paper/manual state changes can explore structure and sequence, with a rehearsed operator and realistic scenario (pp. 42–43). The source now acknowledges retained paper versions as documentation (p. 39), unlike [P04's absolute no-version claim](P04.md#media-and-tool-tradeoffs). Preserve meaningful capture and revision identity. Two hours versus twenty minutes is illustrative; art supplies do not guarantee collaboration or suitability. Reject a blanket gut-reactions-only research rule (p. 41), compulsory three–five-person hallway validation (p. 42), or the suggestion a designer belongs in the wrong industry for preferring a low-tech method (p. 37).

Wizard of Oz can use manual work or staged media to demonstrate an idea (pp. 44–46). **Design-pro application:** disclose the simulation and distinguish demonstrated UX from available automation. Do not sell nonexistent backend capability or report staged actions as live functionality. The IDEO smart-car, Elmo and Groupon stories are secondary anecdotes, not performance evidence. P. 45 says pictures show the Elmo story, but original pp. 44–46 contain no such phone-cutout photo; p. 46 instead shows The Point. Do not claim an absent picture was inspected. A manually delivered service may test a business hypothesis but does not prove automated scale. No coupons, email, invitations or external messages were sent.

## Digital tools and existing code

Pp. 48–59 compare slides, HTML/code and specialized tools. Familiarity, actual supported inputs, edit/reuse effort, collaboration and export limits are useful selection criteria. Feature claims about named products, cloud requirements, market standing, free services and imports remain historical and unverified-current. No tool inherently offers a perfect balance or collaboration without compromise. Code may expose semantic structure (p. 52) and support reuse, but HTML is not automatically a component architecture or native app, and it requires a compatible browser (p. 53). Thirty minutes is not a productivity benchmark. The ZURB Verify 80-sketch/two-week/two-week account (pp. 54–55) is an example rather than a promised schedule, causal proof or quota.

## Usability evidence and task design

Pp. 60–75 support considering users/tasks from the outset, gathering relevant evidence, representative recruitment and careful task wording. A broken or incomplete prototype can still reveal meaningful problems; record the limit rather than universally invalidate all observations (pp. 17, 60). P. 17's five qualitative/at-least-twenty quantitative counts and p. 25's minimum five do not establish general sufficiency or statistical significance. Sample planning depends on the question, design, variation and desired precision; a threshold alone provides no guarantee. See [research guidance](../concepts/research.md), including its distinctions between walkthroughs and real-user studies.

A fictional persona is a communication aid, not an extra participant or evidence. Adding invented life details does not improve predictive validity (pp. 61–62). Use supported task/context characteristics and label assumptions. User/job stories p. 63 help express circumstances and motivation, but a template or causal-sounding sentence does not establish actual causality. Experience maps pp. 64–65 can connect touchpoints and evidence; triangulation does not automatically fill every gap. Card sorting explores grouping/labels; tree tasks explore findability; interviews provide accounts, not necessarily observed task performance (pp. 65–66). Domain experience can matter without making age, language, geography or access needs irrelevant (p. 67).

Direct/scenario and open/closed task distinctions (pp. 68–69) are options. Multiple valid routes can still have clear success criteria; a scenario is not universally superior or guaranteed to make participants forget the session. For discovery/findability tests, describe the goal without naming the intended control. Moderation can help distinguish an unfinished route from user confusion, but intervention must be recorded and cannot count as unaided success (pp. 69–70). Permit stopping when appropriate; do not insist participants continue through harm or distress merely because difficulty is informative.

Fidelity should serve the question, not a compulsory test-every-fidelity/as-often-as-possible schedule, a mandatory medium if only one round is available, or a guarantee high fidelity is always more expensive (pp. 71–73). Use relevant readable content, meaningful images/icons and believable sample data (p. 74); retain actual intended masking where it is part of the product's behavior rather than exposing sensitive values to seem realistic. Do not automatically use the source's sample names or any name as culture-neutral. Realistic-looking data does not guarantee unbiased results. Brainstorming/model walkthroughs, executed browser checks and actual intended-user sessions remain separate (p. 75).

## Practical scope and feedback

Pp. 76–86 reiterate audience/purpose, context, user collaboration, routes, understandable actions and stopping when the prototype has answered its question. A presentation walkthrough can explain features; that same priming would contaminate a claim of unaided discovery (pp. 77–78). Participatory design can inform choices without dismissing users as amateurs or guaranteeing all gaps become visible (pp. 78–79). Reject click-count optimization as a sufficient measure of friction; the source itself disputes a rigid three-click limit (pp. 80–81). Original p. 81 depicts one passenger-count step, not an entire verified booking journey.

Motion is optional; specify a purpose, operation states and relevant preferences rather than promising delight or requiring every app to animate (pp. 81–82). Sketching can help organize ideas but is not a mandatory stage; direct extension of a known component may be enough (pp. 82–84). Prototype consequential questions and retain a stopping condition, but do not use an unsupported 80/20 interaction distribution to ignore low-frequency failures or recovery (p. 85). A top-fifth-only rule cannot establish adequate coverage.

## History is not a present platform rule

Pp. 87–98 discuss Agile/Lean, a timeline and forecasts. Retain collaborative iteration, explicit hypotheses and appropriate evidence; the how-versus-why division is a simplified account, not an exclusive definition. Continuous research does not prohibit research before building (p. 90). Gold-standard, market popularity, static-design extinction and future compulsory prototyping claims are historical advocacy. The timeline's first-ever dates, IBM/RAD attribution and Google-policy/cowboy-coding causal account are not verified and are not adopted as history lessons or engineering policy. The Spiral image p. 93 is conceptual, not this project's measured cost/progress. Single-goal microinteractions can be specified by trigger/rules/feedback (pp. 96–97), while detail polish does not replace complete task flow.

## Historical import and interaction tutorials

Pp. 99–121 teach Photoshop→UXPin import and a small marketing page; pp. 122–132 repeat Sketch import with a scroll reveal. No tutorial was run. Photoshop CC 2014/Sketch 3 compatibility, layer preservation, eleven triggers/twenty actions, trials and sharing are unverified-current claims. Original preview/video thumbnails pp. 101 and 123 are static, not videos watched. P. 100's index names Sketch inside the Photoshop chapter. The pre/post Sketch pictures pp. 125/128 use differing imagery, so they do not prove lossless import of the same file. A Save dialog (pp. 105, 127) is not saved/reloaded verification.

Useful abstraction from pp. 107–110: specify **trigger → action → affected element/state**, including system events such as page load; distinguish movement destination from delta and transition timing from network wait. The chart/task/event selector illustrations do not prove live data or correct tab behavior.

Pp. 111–114 stack default/hover buttons and wire scroll actions. Original p. 112's paired screenshots both say Mouse in / Show, despite prose describing showing on entry and hiding on exit; p. 113 subsequently shows Mouse out / Hide and p. 114 Mouse out / Show default. Copy the intended state contract, not the inconsistent panel recipe. In implementation use one semantic operable control with suitable focus/hover states and a valid destination where practical; do not require hover to expose the only working action. Three hundred milliseconds is a tutorial parameter, not a standard.

Pp. 115–116 hide a form until scrolling to 700px. Essential fields must remain reachable with keyboard, direct entry, zoom, alternative viewports and no optional animation; fixed offsets are not responsive visibility rules. P. 117 hides the email caption on focus; preserve a persistent understandable label and programmatic association instead. Pp. 118–119 show a click hiding the form and displaying thanks, without demonstrating validation, request, failure or durable success. Label a simulated submission honestly; an actual submission needs its actual outcome and recovery. Typeable controls and Tab support claimed by the vendor do not prove the resulting form's accessibility.

Pp. 129–131 hide/fix a display header and reveal it after 100px with a 300ms fade. Original p. 130's screenshot still shows Scrolled To 0 while prose requests 100. Neither is a universal design value. Avoid hiding essential title/navigation; define fallback and reduced-motion behavior when relevant. Comments/live presentation screens (pp. 121, 132) are reviewer tooling, not product controls. Closing p. 133 is promotion. Both local demos should implement only the needed behavior, disclose simulation, and keep verification/rationale in companion records.
