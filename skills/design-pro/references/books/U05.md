Original filename: `User Interface/Tactical UI Design Patterns.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match where printed. Pages 61–103 are primarily screenshot examples; their small extracted text is not the full content. The gallery was inspected as original-page renders, using labeled four-page detail sheets and larger source images where relevant.

## Patterns are ideas whose fit still needs evaluation

The guide distinguishes a recurring solution from a particular component/plugin implementing it (pp. 7–28). Breadcrumbs communicate a hierarchy; a carousel component is one implementation of image navigation. Reuse can support recognition, internal consistency and communication without requiring the same aesthetics everywhere. Familiarity is not proof that a pattern is best, technically reliable, accessible or understood by every audience. Ten-to-twenty seconds is not a universal first-impression deadline.

The hotel example exposes a mismatch between a thumbnail that appears actionable and the actual detail route (pp. 8–12). Repeated behavior teaches expectations, but making users learn a broken expectation is not the preferred solution. Use understandable cues and keep the actual operation consistent. Visited/unvisited color can convey history, but arbitrary green/gray choices do not guarantee recognition or sufficient contrast (p. 11).

Proximity groups hotel image/title/description into a discrete result; the pictured alternative makes the units clearer (pp. 13–16). The source also invokes Hick's law and psychological/visual mechanisms without evidence sufficient to prove each specific recommendation. Do not turn these passages into universal timing formulas. Preserve the distinction between actual capability and the cue indicating it, as in [X05](X05.md#capability-signifier-and-expectation).

The p. 15 color terminology is inconsistent with [U02](U02.md#distinguish-palette-relationships-from-usable-contrast): it calls adjacent hues complementary and a single opposite the maximum possible contrast. Do not adopt this vocabulary or emotion guarantees. Explicit color-model relationships and actual luminance/role checks are more useful than “bright rises above dark.”

The user-perspective questions are useful: where am I, what matters, what can I act on, where can I go, how can I close this, and is work still pending (pp. 16–20)? Collect examples by the problem they solve. The source's library/vendor counts and recommendations are historical, not required services or evidence of current quality.

## Make the process and exceptions concrete

A broad “search, compare, choose” flow hides entry routes, criteria, comparison, confirmation and failure decisions (pp. 29–39). Prototype the uncertainty with relevant content. The hotel examples explore a shortlist/removal view, a map view and structured availability inputs. These are alternatives, not proof that hotel keyword search is useless. A map also needs a usable way to navigate the surrounding page. Do not select a particular external API merely because the book mentions one.

The claim that no reason exists to skip lo-fi prototyping is too rigid for a narrow repair or already well-specified task (p. 29). Choose the representation/fidelity that answers the question. A prototype is not automatically cheaper, a code prototype is not automatically the final product, and a walkthrough does not replace relevant user observation.

The author's portrayal of developers as responsible only for implementation conflicts with the value later assigned to their questions and insight (pp. 34–39). Define behavior and constraints collaboratively rather than excluding a role. Not every decision can be settled upfront. Observe workarounds, uncertainty and relevant comparison tasks; keep assisted performance distinct from unaided success. Five participants is a heuristic, not sufficiency for every claim (p. 54).

## Reuse with enough meaning and explicit variants

Gray boxes can leave irrelevant visuals open, but can also conceal whether an item is an input, image or action (pp. 40–47). Use enough realistic content, labels and interaction to make the tested task interpretable. Reusable/synchronized components can reduce update drift; inspect intentional exceptions before propagating a change to every page. Sharing a pattern name such as “hero” or “modal” does not specify its content, states, focus, closing or recovery behavior.

Build a searchable library around goals and actual paths, then record the conditions, representation and behavior of candidate patterns (pp. 48–58). Inspiration is not a requirement to copy another product's assets or exact styling. The “remove unwanted hotels” example explicitly admits that it may fail (p. 53), a useful qualification of the earlier claim that patterns are proven quantities. Brand color/type, shape and illustration can vary while the underlying task remains recognizable. “Unless/except” may indicate a meaningful variant, not an automatic instruction to break consistency. Color-only required-field/status examples need textual/programmatic support.

## Application to both modes

For the game page, an image gallery can support choosing a screenshot to inspect, but a static readable section may suffice. For chat, a conversation list, composer, pending response and retry must have explicit behavior; a reused component name does not define those states. Keep the chosen pattern's problem, context, alternatives and observed verification beside the project rationale. These applications remain design hypotheses until the implemented flows are exercised and, where appropriate, studied with intended users.
