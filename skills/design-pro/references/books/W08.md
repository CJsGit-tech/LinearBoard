# W08 — Web Design Book of Trends 2013–2014

**Source:** UXPin; Marcin Treder, Robert Warych and Sebastian Witman (p. 2), historical 2013–2014 edition. Exact filename: `Web Design/Web Design Book of Trends 2013-2014.pdf`. All PDF text and all 187 original pages inspected. Body folios match PDF pages. Extraction contains ligature-code artifacts and little/no text on many gallery pages; originals supplied the actual cover, layouts, examples and chapter wording. The gallery presents selected screenshots, often several states per page, not live sites or comparative user research. No depicted animation, input, synchronization, analytics, consent behavior or commercial outcome was executed.

## How to use this historical gallery

The preface reports selecting trends from thousands of sites but supplies no reproducible sampling method, task tests or outcome comparison (pp. 3–4). Its claims that design finally became user-centered, flat design represents universal maturity, or one style will unify the digital world are editorial rhetoric, not evidence (pp. 3–4, 53–54). The 2013/2014 forecasts, device/traffic figures and legal statements are not current requirements. Repeated site appearances across this library do not provide independent corroboration.

Treat each gallery as a set of candidate representations. Ask what users need to understand or do, whether the controls remain recognizable, what information survives different inputs/layouts, and what actual testing would decide. These are design-pro synthesis questions, not measured conclusions from the screenshots.

## Content and narrative

### Content hierarchy — PDF pp. 5–20

The source puts useful content in service of a goal. Retain relevance and hierarchy without equating content only with photography or excluding actions and behavior. A person's purpose can include exploring design itself; the text's universal claim that visitors never admire layout is too strong.

Originals show 1948 London's irregular story mosaic and calendar, Kahuna's centered introduction/project panels, D'Angelico's large guitar and history/dealer sections, TED-Ed's lessons/tour/registration, Oz's scene-based entrance, TCM's colored silhouettes and programme, Tour de France's year/map/jersey views, Polygon's editorial tiles and review, BBC's dense sections, GOOD's signup/content/overlay, True's mixed feed, The Verge's multiscale headlines, National Academy's visit/class information, Google Science Fair's voting/events/FAQ and Glamour's editorial pages (pp. 6–20). This range contradicts any inference that content-first means one sparse layout. Historic news, prices, statistics and claims pictured within these pages were not independently verified.

**Application:** establish what a unit represents and its reading/action priority before copying a grid. Provide visible heading/action meaning when imagery alone would be ambiguous. Large editorial density and sparse product presentation can each suit a task; neither proves usability by appearance.

### Storytelling — PDF pp. 21–36

The opening proposes meaning and persuasion through a core story; that is a communication option, not proof that every narrative is authentic or persuasive (p. 21). Dadaab pairs community photographs with topic navigation and stories; Browser Awareness separates nontechnical/technical explanations; NASA: Prospect uses an illustrated astronaut sequence; Distance to Mars translates scale into a long spatial representation (pp. 22–25). MediaBOOM uses scenes and portfolio access; Orange offers history highlights/timeline; The Power Inside pairs episodes with products; Snipcart alternates explanation, code, service and pricing panels (pp. 26–29). Black in History combines a name index and profiles, YourTour combines scenes/maps/stage detail, Inception uses labeled character circles and timeline layers, Atlassian uses an illustrated work-interruption infographic, These Are Things uses recurring characters/products, Reverend Danger uses characters for service/contact, and Black Ram offers bottle/history/media sections (pp. 30–36).

These illustrate sequencing, direct indexes, diagrams and multiple paths. They do not verify historical/scientific claims, infographic percentages, donation impact or product performance. Keep optional exploration distinct from finding known information. Do not require long scrolling, a demographic split, a video or a fictionalized brand story for every task. Diagrams need meaningful text equivalents; color-coded characters alone would not establish access.

## Adaptation and style

### Responsive examples — PDF pp. 37–52

Retain designing for supported contexts and content that changes available width. Reject “only responsive design” can serve all devices, and do not reuse 2012/2013 traffic/TV figures as current evidence (p. 37). Browser width, physical resolution, input method and user context are different concerns.

Northbound's project grid, Café Evoke's menu/contact, Trent Walton's article, TIME's news/article, This Is Responsive's resource lists, Skinny Ties' collection/product and Indochino's clothing/product are shown at several widths (pp. 38–44). United Pixelworkers reduces product-grid columns; Five Simple Steps reflows books/detail; Justin Aguilar and Teehan+Lax adapt portfolio/content panels; MRY, BEAM, Squarespace and HubSpot recompose hero/service/case-study content (pp. 45–52). Some screenshots show different stories/hero states, so they are not always matched-content comparisons. Static desktop/phone pairs do not establish intermediate reflow, keyboard access, device performance, complete task parity or synchronization.

**Application:** use actual text, product variations, images and interaction states to select breakpoints. Keep necessary actions/content reachable; check reading/focus order and meaningful crops. Do not equate a pictured phone frame with an implemented native app. See [responsive and continuity synthesis](../concepts/responsive-and-continuity.md).

### Flat styling — PDF pp. 53–69

The illustrated gallery includes SpellTower's colored quadrants, Windows Phone's product scenes/tiles, Buffalo's hexagonal portfolio, TheIssland's identity mosaic, I Think I Might's overlapping photo cards/map, the Shape Christmas calendar, TVLcorps' service grid, Invoisse's notification signup, Coloured Lines' colored navigation, Ecology Center's topic imagery, Numbrs' account/security marketing, Palace's project tiles, Wistia's play shapes, PSD2HTML's services and Playground's agency photos/illustration (pp. 55–69).

Actual examples mix photography, text, depth and ornament; “flat” is not a guarantee of simple behavior, small files or clear controls. A lock graphic and “safe as a bank” copy do not prove security (p. 65). Invoisse's pictured notification result does not prove delivery (p. 62). Calendar order may serve optional discovery, but must not be imposed on a task requiring chronology (p. 60). Do not infer a current platform rule or necessary aesthetic from adoption claims about Microsoft, Google or Apple.

## Motion and navigation

### Parallax — PDF pp. 70–85

The source proposes apparent depth from relative movement; screenshots cannot show its velocity, comfort or input behavior (p. 70). Yep illustrates a meeting sequence, Tinké presents product/metric features, MoreSleep uses red/black agency sections, Quechua uses a lookbook journey, Mahédine Yahia uses a city/portfolio, Shadow uses a dream/phone story, LOFT uses community stories and Gramercy Park uses interior scenes (pp. 71–78). Sløret uses paint/texture, Andrew McCarthy repeats a cat across colored sections, Kilfish uses an elaborate composition, Life of Pi combines film scenes/explanation, Andreas Kleiberg uses portfolio/detail overlays, Every Last Drop uses a daily-life water story, and Madwell uses a floating agency scene (pp. 79–85).

Do not convert attractive static arrangements into proof of smooth motion, learning, memory or health effects. Tinké's pictured metrics are not medical validation. A normal scroll with useful content can be sufficient; provide usable paths without nonessential motion and avoid taking scroll control away from the person.

### Background video — PDF pp. 86–101

The chapter claims emotional engagement from large video; no comparison or measurement is supplied (p. 86). HKI and Whiteboard layer identity/copy over scenes, Joe San and Sonia show clothing, Jobs shows film promotion, Trask uses fictional corporate/science imagery, MojoTech shows a brand toolkit, Langtidsmodnet shows food provenance/recipes, Tool shows production work, L'attrape rêve shows art/gallery, Staring Is Caring shows a campaign/product, W Eyewear shows fashion scenes, Prettybird shows production cases, Sketchin shows device/product contexts and Raymond Weil shows music/watch craft (pp. 87–101).

Keep the medium useful and the overlaid information legible across relevant frames. A paused still does not establish accessible moving content. Distinguish fictional campaign content, brand claims and actual evidence. No background video is required for the game page; user-controlled media and equivalent information may better serve the task. Consult [audio](../current-accessibility.md#audio-control), [moving content](../current-accessibility.md#moving-and-auto-updating-content) and [text alternatives](../current-accessibility.md#non-text-content).

### Nonstandard navigation — PDF pp. 102–117

Retain the questions of current location and available destinations. Reject the chapter's unsupported ranking that bottom navigation is generally more usable and keyboard navigation less usable (p. 102). [Keyboard access](../current-accessibility.md#keyboard-operation) remains an explicit concern.

Vespillo visibly offers mouse or keyboard navigation; Cascade uses gauges/switches and content windows; Adidas careers uses Stores/Fit/Jobs/Shine; Beoplay H6 uses product sections and a side indicator; CO3 uses a spatial room/episode interface; Diesel adds product hotspots; Nerval uses portfolio panels; Interactive Ear uses spatial parts/hotspots; Le Rockwood offers lower navigation; Online Department uses cases; World Baking Day offers baking-level selection; Design Embraced has a lower control bar; Hot Dot labels mouse-wheel/keyboard alternatives; Oakley shows a feature list/product details; ELI uses categorized columns (pp. 103–117).

These are optional representations. A thematic control should still have an understandable name, state, destination and recovery path. A depicted keyboard hint does not prove implementation works. Ear diagrams/product demonstrations need other access as appropriate; a level selector is not a validated measure of a user's cooking ability.

### Fixed navigation — PDF pp. 118–133

The source favors fixed bars as a coming standard; that prediction does not make them compulsory (p. 118). DesignTAXI illustrates a reduced yellow header; The Original Classy Broad, UXPin's blog, The Secret Handshake, Derek Boateng, LOOP, Kings of Summer, Octave & Octave jobs, Carlos Molina, Rezo Zero, Adam Rudzki, ONY, DubbelFrisss, Case 3D and Carrera show persistent-looking header/footer arrangements across gallery states (pp. 119–133).

They vary in available destinations, density, image contrast and task: editorial filtering/search, projects, jobs, contact and product collections. Fixed access trades repeated travel against occupied screen space and possible obstruction. Test actual scrolling, focus visibility, zoom, soft keyboard and target positions before claiming success. The screenshots do not prove fixed positioning or transitions remain correct throughout use.

## Grouping, type and clarity

### Metro grids — PDF pp. 134–149

The name/trademark anecdote and popularity assertions are historical, not current legal or platform guidance (p. 134). Kitchen mixes projects/text; UGSMAG and ReadWrite mix editorial units; Gadget Flow presents product discovery and a signed-out state; Juliana mixes bikes/story/shop; Mustafa Demirkent uses a portfolio/detail overlay; Pulco uses editorial/photo blocks; Ale de Carvalho and Control Films use work/media; Escape Flight combines destination criteria with photo/price tiles; Etch mixes team/projects; Coimbra combines a building diagram, visit information and tours; Hommard combines clothing and product detail; the next page shows a theatre news/ticket grid; MVA mixes music/video/event units (pp. 135–149).

**Caption error:** p. 148 repeats Hommard's title/URL, but its actual screenshot is branded **NOWY TEATR** and contains theatre performances/tickets. Do not cite it as clothing e-commerce or assume duplicated caption means duplicated screenshot. The preface's example counts likewise do not establish unique sites or independent evidence.

Choose a coherent content unit and hierarchy before a tile skin. Maintain meaningful reading order when rearranging units. A signed-out state needs understandable next steps; playful dead-face imagery alone is insufficient (p. 138). Destination prices/product features remain historical unverified content, not recommendations.

### Mixed typography — PDF pp. 150–165

The source celebrates mixed fonts as identity; retain purposeful hierarchy without requiring typographic complexity (p. 150). Forefathers uses a frontier motif, Zegna combines display/editorial roles, Skybox overlays bold type and work, Chris Wilhite puts labels on product imagery, Timmy Tompkins uses comic-like display, BTL uses mountains/type, Fontwalk displays specimen styles, Jacksonville Art Walk mixes event/map/participation information, Wicked Palate uses menu hierarchy, Production Kitchen combines illustrated text with drag/keyboard/Lite-version invitations, CIRQ uses vineyard lettering, Cast Iron uses vintage print, Barrelhouse uses menu/location typography, Wild Blue uses a curved statement and Al Taglio uses handwritten restaurant copy (pp. 151–165).

Decorative titles can express a fictional game's world while body text and controls remain plain and legible. Do not imitate every type style, encode essential text only in images, or treat type effects as proof of clarity. Production Kitchen's visible alternate-access hints are useful to notice, but no alternate path was executed here (p. 160). Check actual font/script/content/contrast and text resizing.

## Cookie warnings and final qualification

The blanket legal assertion p. 182 is not adopted as current or universally applicable law. The preface promises four examples, but pp. 183–185 supply three gallery pages: True's small notice, Lexus's larger Accept/Change Settings surface and Satorisan's thin notice over a campaign. True's text treats browsing as acceptance; that wording is not a reusable consent rule. A visible dismissal, settings link or acceptance button does not establish what scripts actually load, meaningful choice or legal adequacy. These are historical visual observations only; no present legal compliance assessment or recommendation is made.

For any real consent task, determine actual technology, data purposes and applicable current requirements before design; do not copy these screenshots as certification. The demos do not require adding third-party tracking to illustrate design.

The closing cautions against unnecessary movement (pp. 186–187). Retain serving the purpose, while rejecting the suggestion that restraint alone guarantees a good result. For both modes, explain what was observed, test actual task behavior and distinguish local/browser evidence from native, assistive-technology or human-user verification.
