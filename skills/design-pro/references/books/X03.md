# X03 — 8 Steps to Building Features Your Users Actually Want

**Source:** Craig Morrison, Usability Hour; distributed with UserTesting branding (pp. 14–15). Original filename: `User Experience/8 Steps to Building Features Your Users Actually Want.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages. No publication date is established. The guide explicitly adapts a feature-building methodology from *Running Lean* (p. 4); that book was not supplied or independently reviewed here.

## Feature scope and the problem behind a request

New features add navigation, maintenance, support and attention costs; expanding a Twitter-focused tool into Facebook support illustrates a possible change to its value proposition (pp. 3–4). Require a reason to invest, rather than treating every request as a commitment (p. 5).

Find the source of the request and discuss the underlying problem before deciding on a feature (p. 6). Do not copy the guide's confrontational language or try to win an argument against the requester. Design-pro application: ask what the person is trying to accomplish and what makes their current route difficult; a requested solution is evidence to investigate, not necessarily the only solution.

The literal “default no” framing is a scope-control heuristic, not justification to dismiss small populations or essential accessibility/recovery requirements. Low usage does not establish low importance. These qualifications are design-pro judgment.

## Test a useful outcome with a suitable prototype

Define an expected outcome before building (p. 7). The example predicts a 50% increase in session duration when adding another network. That is an illustrative hypothesis, not a target to reuse: if users want to finish faster, longer sessions could be worse. Choose observable task success and appropriate guardrails rather than rewarding activity alone.

Create an inexpensive, changeable front end before unnecessary backend work (p. 8). Match realism to the question; the source asks for realistic presentation, but not every navigation question requires polished visuals. Explain the participant's scenario and observe their use, then refine (p. 9). Avoid teaching the proposed solution or treating positive reactions as proof of demand; these research cautions qualify the source's positive-response gate.

## Staged evidence and decisions

The guide distinguishes initial feedback from use in a real context during a partial rollout (p. 10). Observe whether people discover and use the feature to solve the original problem, rather than assuming a successful guided demo will generalize.

After release, collect outcomes and compare with the expectation (pp. 11–12). The source's one-or-two-week example does not define a sufficient sample, seasonality window or statistical test. Before/after changes alone do not establish causality; concurrent changes and cohort differences matter. It allows reconsidering the original goal if other useful outcomes appear, but such outcomes should be identified as exploratory rather than silently redefining success.

Removing a feature is not an automatic action just because a metric missed a target. Assess obligations, rare but important tasks, data preservation and affected users before proposing retirement. The book's categorical claim that unmeasured improvements imply harm (p. 12) is too strong. Measurement can be incomplete.

## Visual examples and source limits

The session-duration chart (p. 7) illustrates a proposed before/after change, not reported experimental data. Cartoon feature trials (pp. 4–6, 12) are persuasive metaphors, not evaluation frameworks. The p. 13 flow summarizes the eight stages; it does not require shipping, emailing users or deploying anything when the current request only authorizes a prototype. The reference to success defined in “step 2” on p. 12 is a numbering error; success is step 3 on p. 7.

This is practical process advice with commercial promotion and no detailed research methods. It supports lean scope and outcome-based evaluation, not universal feature-removal thresholds. For both demos, record what is simulated, test the interaction, and keep live service integration outside scope unless actually needed and authorized.
