# X09 — The Basics of User Experience Design

**Source:** Interaction Design Foundation, *The Basics of User Experience Design*, preface by Mads Soegaard (pp. 1–2). Original filename: `User Experience/The Basics of UX Design.pdf`. All locators are PDF pages; visible folios match. No precise publication date is established. Predictions for 2020 and older device/market examples situate this as historical introductory material. Chapter promotions and testimonials are not independent evidence.

## UX, usefulness and evidence

The opening treats UX as broader than an isolated interface and connects investigation, communication and implementation (pp. 4–7). Its reduction of project success to a single factor—how users feel—is too narrow; later chapters themselves include utility, access and task outcomes. A method cannot guarantee success, as the opening eventually acknowledges.

The seven-factor honeycomb includes useful, usable, findable, credible, desirable, accessible and valuable (pp. 21–26). Use it to find missing considerations, not as a scored formula or proof that every dimension has equal weight. Enjoyment can be a legitimate purpose for a game; usefulness is not limited to utilitarian work (p. 22). Credibility includes accurate information and dependable behavior (p. 23). The Porsche/Skoda desirability comparison is the author's broad cultural judgment, not evidence of every audience's preferences (p. 24).

Accessibility concerns a range of abilities and can improve broader use (p. 25). Do not transfer its 2010 population figure or jurisdictional legal claims into current requirements. Product value involves user and organizational needs (p. 25), but a simple price/problem-cost comparison does not establish adoption or business viability.

## Iterative problem framing

Empathize, define, ideate, prototype and test are modes that can overlap and recur, not compulsory sequential gates (pp. 10–19). The p. 18 diagram explicitly connects testing back to user understanding, new ideas and problem definition. Challenge the initial framing and consider alternatives within actual constraints. The trapped-truck story is expressly illustrative and may not be a real incident (pp. 12–14); do not present it as research evidence.

The contrast between design thinking and “solely scientific” inquiry is a simplified teaching narrative, not a reliable boundary between disciplines. Nor does a five-mode framework replace domain expertise. Corporate adoption, origin stories and course claims in these pages have not been independently verified here.

## Usability and recovery

The usability chapter emphasizes specific users, goals and context, then describes effectiveness, efficiency, engagement, error tolerance and learning (pp. 28–34). These are useful evaluation lenses; the five characteristics are attributed to Whitney Quesenberry rather than being the quoted standard itself.

The PDF prints **ISO 9421-11** on p. 28. The primary [ISO catalog](https://www.iso.org/standard/63500.html), checked 2026-10-25, identifies the usability definitions/concepts standard as **ISO 9241-11:2018**. This review checked its catalog/abstract, not the full paid standard, and does not claim conformity. Keep that correction separate from the source's wording.

Clear language, useful grouping, alternate routes where helpful and appropriate input support can improve completion (pp. 29–31). The sixth-grade readability target is an English-oriented heuristic; language, expertise and the task determine the right level. Do not restrict a valid input format merely to fit a simplistic example. Efficiency involves task effort and resources, not only click count or raw speed despite the chapter's narrow phrasing.

Prevent foreseeable errors and offer undo, redo or a clear recovery route where meaningful (p. 32). Learnability can benefit from existing mental models, but a virtual control need not imitate a physical button to work (p. 33). A highly usable social feature can still be irrelevant to a payment task: utility and usability are different questions (pp. 33–34).

## Interviewing and interpretation

Interviews reveal reported experience; memory, social context and the question can distort accounts (pp. 36–40). Ask about actual behavior and needs, explain purpose/data use, listen, adapt the guide and respect agreed time (pp. 38–40). A user is not required to design the solution, but suggestions can still reveal an unmet need. Do not dismiss them categorically.

The notetaking diagram separates description, inference and evaluation (p. 37). Preserve those distinctions: seeing a pause is different from inferring frustration or judging an entire relationship. Its proposed two-researcher setup can help divide attention, but recording is not the only possible way to conduct a solo interview; choose a feasible consented method. Eye contact and dress conventions are contextual, not universal requirements.

The claim that contextual interviews almost guarantee accurate responses conflicts with the chapter's own caveats (p. 38). Observation also requires interpretation. Word clouds may summarize word frequency but do not establish priority or causality; keep evidence traceable when reporting themes (p. 40).

## Select research for its output

The methods chapter lists card sorting, expert review, eye tracking, field studies, usability tests, remote tests and personas (pp. 43–49). They are not interchangeable or all necessary:

- Card sorting explores grouping/terminology; the pictured physical cards show categorization, not proof that the resulting navigation is usable (pp. 43–44).
- Expert review can suggest risks and further questions; the text warns against letting it replace user testing (p. 45).
- Eye tracking observes gaze, not understanding, preference or task success by itself. The source's broad affordability/reliability claims are not current procurement evidence (pp. 45–46).
- Field work can reveal context, and task observation can expose a specific breakdown (pp. 46–47). Neither is universally the strongest or irrefutable evidence; task design, recruitment, environment and interpretation matter.
- Remote testing may broaden reach and preserve some context, but does not automatically reproduce ordinary use or outperform a lab (pp. 47–48).
- Research-derived personas summarize findings; an invented ideal user is not new research evidence (p. 48).

## Interaction across input, time and context

The five-dimension model covers words, visual representations, physical objects/space, time and behavior (pp. 52–56). Use it to inspect an interaction's label, cues, input method, progress/resumption and actual response. It is a thinking aid, not a reason every interaction needs sound or animation. Error messages should explain the problem or help correction, and feedback should follow the action (pp. 54–55). The marketing claim that a few milliseconds decide quality is not a measured timing requirement (p. 56).

## Mobile web qualifications

The mobile chapter explicitly calls its advice guidelines rather than hard rules (p. 58). Consider task setting, input, viewport and interruptions; a phone may also be used at a desk (pp. 58–59). Simplify navigation, labels and unnecessary entry while retaining essential capability, and preserve data across connection breaks (pp. 60–62).

Do not adopt its 30×30-pixel target rule as a current/native requirement; use [current web criteria](../current-accessibility.md#target-size) and the actual platform. Its responsive/device versus adaptive/server distinction is oversimplified, and separate mobile/desktop sites are not mandatory. Saving important work for a future desktop visit can exclude a mobile-only user. “Permanent sign-in is safer on phones” is an unsupported generalization (p. 61); authentication depends on the task and threat context, not screen size.

The p. 61 map shows subscription-style penetration categories reaching 125%, not a direct measurement of connection stability at a user's location. It supports awareness of variation, not today's coverage claims. Preserve continuity across widths without assuming a separate full-site switch is always needed (p. 62).

## Information visualization and honesty

Choose representation for the question: written directions support a route, while a map adds surrounding spatial context (pp. 65–67). A schematic Underground map emphasizes connections rather than geographic fidelity (pp. 67–68). Its suitability depends on the task; it cannot be assumed usable by everyone.

Visual exploration can suggest a relationship without explaining the cause (p. 69). The map legend concerns age-adjusted death rates per population, so the source's loose references to disease “frequency” should not turn rates into raw counts. The two-line graph is explicitly titled **Geometric Brownian Motion** with different parameters; the imagined methane experiment in the paragraph is not its established provenance (p. 70). Similar-looking trends do not confirm a causal link.

The discussion of selective omission to persuade, including hypothetical health/placebo percentages (p. 68), does not justify hiding material comparison data. Those numbers are illustrative, not medical findings. Design-pro chooses accurate labels, denominators, uncertainty and relevant counterevidence over a misleading impression; this is deliberate qualification of the source's permissive framing.

## Scope and application

Job/salary claims, institution roles, technology pricing, course scarcity and membership offers are historical promotions (pp. 2, 7–9, 19–20, 26–27, 34–35, 41–42, 49–51, 56–57, 63–64, 71–73). They are not necessary design-pro dependencies. For both demos, identify the task, keep observed behavior separate from interpretation, preserve useful work and verify the interface across relevant inputs. A fictional project and internal test remain distinct from participant research.
