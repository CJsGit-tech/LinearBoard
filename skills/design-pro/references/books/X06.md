# X06 — Getting Started With UX Design Process & Documentation

**Source:** Guiseppe Getto and Jerry Cao, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 3, 5–6; first name spelling follows this PDF). Original filename: `User Experience/Getting Started with UX Design Process & Documentation.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages.

## Learn through use and reflection

The guide pairs theory, practice and reflection rather than equating collecting resources with expertise (pp. 6–9, 30–31). Keep a categorized library so a current problem can retrieve relevant advice; revisit assumptions when application reveals a gap. This supports design-pro's concept references and demos, not a claim that reading permanently retrains the model.

Stages—research, prototyping, testing and maintenance—use different skills and often recur (pp. 7–8). Do not turn this teaching taxonomy into a rigid sequence. The source recommends hypothetical and client projects as practice (pp. 11–12); the fictional demo briefs are the former and must not masquerade as validated client requirements.

## Link people, situations and paths

Preliminary work establishes the project, available tools and collaboration expectations; contextual inquiry investigates wants and needs in the setting of use (pp. 10–12). A user's setting is evidence to investigate, not a persona detail to invent.

Personas, stories, scenarios and flows serve different purposes (pp. 13–15). The story table joins a role, action and desired result. A scenario adds environment and circumstances; a flow maps possible paths through the product. The persona and signup/loading/onboarding illustrations repeat examples in [X01](X01.md#connected-minimal-documentation) and [X04](X04.md#verification-methods-are-different), not independent studies.

The assertion that all research documents grow out of a persona (p. 13) is too broad and sits uneasily with personas being derived from research. Preserve the source of evidence and choose only artifacts that help the decision. People and task variation matter more than a fictional demographic profile.

## Plan for content maintenance early

Information architecture and content strategy affect a product after launch as well as before design (pp. 16–19). Distinguish a sitemap of connections, a taxonomy of controlled terms and actual content repositories. A governance plan can identify goals, owners' updating work, available material and audit cadence; the source lists goals, calendars, repositories and audits (p. 19). Owner assignment is a design-pro application.

The p. 18 sitemap connects page-level structure with prototype detail and repeats the About/Work/Blog/Contact example in [X02](X02.md#connect-content-structure-and-interaction). Its presence is not a mandate to create a sitemap for a single-control correction. For a release page, content upkeep concerns the actual release/platform/action details; for chat, it concerns conversations and useful state labels, without requiring a publishing calendar for the local demo.

## Prototype to answer a question

A prototype simulates enough interaction to evaluate a flow; its fidelity and technological choices need not match the final implementation (pp. 20–23). The guide describes static wireframes, interactive wireframes, UI elements and higher-fidelity mockups, then explicitly permits alternate starting points (p. 23). Its earlier claim that every prototype begins as a static wireframe (p. 21) should therefore not be treated as a required process.

Additional visual detail does not automatically require more controls. Reuse the existing component system and choose fidelity for the problem; a convincing simulation still needs disclosure of service boundaries. These qualifications are design-pro synthesis.

## Evaluate and report with traceability

Contextual inquiry asks about needs; usability work examines whether a product supports a goal (pp. 24–25). Define the question, method, participants and task scenario before the session. Avoid leading the participant through the correct steps, even though the source mentions steps from task analysis. The sample introduction reassures people that the product is being tested and explains the session (p. 26); its fixed hour-long duration is an example, not a requirement.

Communicate what worked, where use failed and useful changes (pp. 26–29). The p. 27 report template asks for the reason, timing, method and key findings. Remove placeholder text and do not invent participant quotes to complete a template. The p. 24 observation matrix can associate issues with particular participants; its colored cells alone do not establish severity or population frequency.

The p. 28 journey template includes goals, expectations, process, experience and potential improvement. It contains repeated “before service” headers and a mostly blank rating area; it is an example scaffold, not reported research. Journeys can include abandonment or failure, despite the text claiming they always end in accomplishing tasks. Record observed paths and uncertainty instead of forcing success into the map.

Refine based on actual findings. “Always test each iteration” (p. 29) is broad practice advice, not justification for redundant full studies on trivial changes. Match verification to risk and distinguish local browser tests from representative-user sessions.

## Evidence limits

The source is an educator's practitioner framework, not a controlled evaluation of all proposed methods. Suggested books and resource sites (pp. 10–11, 32–33) are secondary recommendations; their current availability, author roles, subscriptions and quality have not been verified. The ending advertisement (p. 34) adds no evidence of method effectiveness. The useful outcome is a small set of linked decisions and tested behavior, not a complete set of documents created by habit.
