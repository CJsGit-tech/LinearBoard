# U17 — Winning Over Users With Attractive UI Design

**Source:** UXPin, 2015; Jerry Cao, Matt Ellis and Kamil Zieba (p. 7). Exact filename: `User Interface/Winning Over Users With Attractive UI Design.pdf`. Citations use PDF pages, matching body folios. Running headers incorrectly say *The Designer's Guide to Collaborating With Developers*; do not use that as this book's title. All text pp. 1–41 read; 38 relevant original pages inspected. Exact images are in the separate ledger. No pictured website or ATM was tested.

## Appearance is one part of the experience

The book emphasizes navigation cues, emphasis, feedback, identity, consistency, spacing and credible presentation (pp. 6–21, 24–38). Retain the practical link between an understandable appearance and its function. Do not adopt its claims that effectiveness is only skin-deep, every pixel is subconsciously processed, everyone primarily uses sight, or outside appearance is all that counts (pp. 7–10, 39–40). Nonvisual use and actual capability remain necessary.

The green word WHITE (p. 8) is not an experiment establishing that most people comprehend only its ink color; wording can itself interfere with color identification. The hardware-bandwidth analogy, 0.7% awareness figure, evolutionary/adrenaline explanations and supposed blood-flow effect of red are not established design mechanisms here (pp. 8–9, 17, 27). They are not incorporated as scientific facts or health advice.

ATM anecdotes distinguish perceived appeal from function, but the source later reasons from a conditional relaxation theory to actual performance and a required equal time allocation (pp. 10, 20–21). This does not prove a causal effect for every task or justify a fixed appearance/function time split. The rounded-rectangle argument likewise does not establish improved usability or Apple's reasons. An exact ten-second decision deadline and rigid traditional/cutting-edge personality match are unverified generalizations (pp. 18–20).

## Credibility claim checked against its primary source

The p. 10 claim that 46.1% of all users judged trust **on looks alone** misstates the evidence. Checked **2026-10-25** against [Fogg et al., *How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility?*, 2002 report](https://credibility.stanford.edu/pdf/How_Do_People_Evaluate_a_Web_Site%27s_Credibility_v37.pdf), PDF pp. 4–6, 20, 23–25: 2,684 participants evaluated site pairs; the analysis coded 2,440 comments, and 46.1% mentioned design look. The report includes positive and negative visual judgments. It does not establish appearance as the sole determinant, a current population percentage, actual truth/security, or a guaranteed conversion effect. Only these focused passages were reviewed, not the entire external report.

Use visual clarity alongside accurate claims and behavior. A grouped payment region and lock icon may communicate a promise; they do not establish encryption, confidentiality or successful payment (p. 15). No payment or live service is needed for the demos. Client logos/testimonials/team images must reflect real authorized relationships, rather than fabricated proof (p. 38).

## Cues, emphasis and feedback

Icon-only navigation is not inherently easier or preferred (pp. 11–12). Assess audience recognition and provide usable labels/names where needed; do not assume all metaphors are universal. Red count badges can emphasize activity without ensuring someone notices or knows its meaning. A green action against white needs actual contrast and understandable wording (pp. 12–13).

Hover acknowledgement is different from activation, completion or persistence (pp. 13–14). Keep equivalent access and meaningful state feedback for other inputs. A playful reveal does not guarantee enjoyment or justify encouraging unnecessary interactions.

The seven practices include reducing unnecessary options and explicit/pattern/hidden/metaphorical/negative signifiers (pp. 22–26). Treat these as working vocabulary, not a complete taxonomy. Neither Hick's Law nor the illustrated choice-paralysis curve proves that fewer choices, hidden submenus or a seven-plus-or-minus-two quota is always better. Group by task and test retrieval/recovery. Disabled controls may need explanation; gray text alone is not a complete state model. A pattern icon need not be understood by every person.

Color/mood mappings are possible cultural associations, not physiological guarantees, universal preferences or trust evidence (pp. 27–31). The section calls its list primary colors while also listing orange, green, purple, white and black; this is not a precise color-theory classification. Symmetry categories are composition options; asymmetry need not imply an unstable or edgy product (pp. 31–33).

A relevant human photograph can support a message, but is not always more important than task information or product imagery; “non-stock” origin alone is not a guarantee (p. 34). The paragraph itself later allows suitable stock. Preserve intentional type/component variants and existing meaningful conventions rather than forcing every view into identical visuals (pp. 35–36). Space can group and separate; more space does not force more attention or a luxury perception (pp. 36–37).

## Original visual observations

- Ava's facial composition, the green WHITE, brain drawing, needs pyramid, record player, people/heart/ATM photographs (pp. 6, 8–11, 16, 18–21) illustrate arguments rather than observed user outcomes.
- LinkedIn's envelope/flag/people icons include red counts (p. 12); Opera uses a centered download control (p. 13). Huncwot shows a highlighted service amid four choices (p. 14), not proof of all input paths. Payment-form variants change background/lock appearance (p. 15), not actual protection.
- The Amazon screenshot includes a visible expanded department list despite the text emphasizing hidden submenus (p. 23). Its annotated graph p. 22 is not measured data from the screenshot.
- UXPin signup, Hulu's play triangle, Pinterest's hidden/revealed controls, folder/upload/download/gear icons and Photoshop's disabled menu entries illustrate different signifier uses (pp. 24–26). Actual accessible names/focus and action results remain unverified.
- Playtika, Epic/Lunar Gravity, Massis/Evolve Wealth, WooView/ETQ and Cartelle illustrate colored fields/brand imagery (pp. 27–31), not objective moods. Huncwot, Squarespace, Carlo Barberis and Julie Flogeac repeat U13's balance gallery (pp. 32–33), not independent corroboration.
- Beoplay's person/headphones, Red Hat's branded sections, iMac's product composition and Elite's portraits (pp. 34–36, 39) serve different content contexts. None proves a universal human-face preference. Pp. 40–41 are vendor promotion/trial claims, not current recommendations.

## Application

Give the game concept a coherent identity while accurately communicating gameplay, release assumptions and action results. In chat, keep labels, state and recovery usable regardless of decorative appeal. These are design-pro applications; perceived polish does not establish backend reliability, native execution, accessibility or user-tested success.
