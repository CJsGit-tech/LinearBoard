# M06 — Mobile UI Design Patterns

**Source:** UXPin, *Mobile UI Design Patterns: A Deeper Look at the Hottest Apps Today*, Chris Bank, co-written by Waleed Zuberi; design by Dominik Pacholczyk (PDF pp. 2, 7). Original filename: `Mobile Design/Mobile UI Design Patterns - A Deeper Look At The Hottest Apps Today.pdf`. Publication date is not established by the opening credits. All locators below are PDF pages. The screenshots and product claims are historical, not documentation of current app behavior.

## Select patterns by problem

Patterns describe recurring problems and possible solutions, not ready-made features or code. The opening distinguishes problem, solution, examples, usage and context, then acknowledges that the book concentrates on the first three (pp. 6–9). Evaluate the omitted usage conditions before adopting an example. The closing explicitly rejects a one-size-fits-all solution (p. 134).

## Input and action

- Match the keyboard to expected input; contacts and composition examples contrast phone and text layouts (pp. 16–17). Defaults and autocomplete reduce repeated entry (pp. 18–20). Design-pro qualification: defaults must be visible and correctable; a location-based suggestion does not justify collecting location for every task.
- Let users experience value before registration when the task permits it. Wunderlist associates registration with synchronization and Houzz allows browsing before account commitment (pp. 21–23). Some tasks genuinely require identity or personal data; do not remove necessary authentication merely to imitate the pattern.
- Keep frequent task actions accessible in an action bar (pp. 24–25). Consolidate infrequent actions in an overflow menu when that does not hide the primary task (pp. 53–55). Social sign-in (pp. 26–28) is an example of reducing credential entry, not a security guarantee or a recommendation for a current provider SDK.
- Large, prominent actions can suit a single dominant task, as Shazam illustrates (pp. 29–31). The claimed ideal of 72px is not established as a universal requirement. Actual activation area, neighboring actions, platform and use context still matter.
- Swipe-to-act (pp. 32–34) and swipe-to-browse (pp. 78–79) solve different problems. Do not assign the same movement conflicting meanings in one context; retain a usable alternate route. This qualification is design-pro synthesis, consistent with [M04](M04.md#gestures).
- Expandable search saves space when search is secondary (pp. 45–46); avoid hiding it if search is the main task. Undo can reduce disruptive confirmation for genuinely reversible actions (pp. 47–48), but an unavailable or incomplete undo does not make an irreversible operation safe.

## Navigation and continuity

Walkthroughs and coach marks (pp. 50–52) illustrate contextual teaching, not a requirement to put every user through a tutorial. The Carousel slides show a particular interaction in context. Test whether users can perform the task rather than counting tutorial screens.

The Tinder summary/detail example preserves the selected photo when returning (pp. 58–59). That continuity is more useful than copying its visual style. Morphing controls can express a related inverse action, such as Follow/Unfollow (pp. 60–62); labels and state must still identify the current operation.

Persistent navigation and section headers can preserve orientation in long content (pp. 63–65). Lists (pp. 66–67), popovers (pp. 68–70), drawers (pp. 71–73), contextual links (pp. 74–75) and indexed scrolling (pp. 76–77) offer different access strategies. A drawer still requires prioritization; the source's suggestion that everything can simply be tucked away is not adopted. Its popover examples mix modal and nonmodal presentations, so they do not establish a shared focus or dismissal policy. Design-pro application: check that persistent UI does not cover content and that opening/closing secondary UI preserves the task.

## Social patterns require a social task

Feeds (pp. 81–83), friend lists (pp. 84–85), topic following (pp. 86–88), voting (pp. 89–90), direct messages (pp. 91–92), sharing (pp. 93–94), likes (pp. 95–96) and invitations (pp. 97–98) are options, not a mandatory engagement checklist. Following a topic differs from following a person. A binary reaction is useful only when the required judgment is actually binary. A single sharing entry can reduce chrome without eliminating the choice of destination. Do not add contacts access, social graphs or referrals to the chatbot demo without a user need; this is design-pro scope judgment.

## Content and interface states

- Full-screen reading/media (pp. 100–102) can reduce surrounding chrome while retaining an understandable way out. Interactive tags and map markers associate detail with its spatial object (pp. 103–104, 111–112); do not use a map as decoration when location is irrelevant.
- Inline expansion of message metadata (pp. 105–106) and secondary hidden information (pp. 121–122) reduce repeated detail. Keep information essential to deciding or recovering visible; hiding failures to obtain a cleaner transcript would misuse the pattern. This is design-pro synthesis.
- Shape and transparency are visual devices, not sufficient interaction semantics (pp. 107–110). The source calls circles optimal touch targets, then admits their benefit over square thumbnails is unclear (p. 108). It supplies no basis for a universal circle rule.
- User-defined grouping (pp. 113–114), full-bleed imagery (pp. 115–116), grids (pp. 117–118) and independently useful cards (pp. 119–120) address different organizational needs. Image-heavy grids suit visual browsing; they do not establish that dense text comparisons should use tiles. Full bleed is not evidence that all whitespace should disappear.
- Empty states explain why there is no content and what the user can do (pp. 123–124). The YPlan and Airbnb examples connect the blank view to tickets or trips rather than displaying an unexplained zero.
- Direct manipulation can show the result in place: filters, editable tasks and spreadsheet cells (pp. 125–128). Dragging reorders items in the current context (pp. 129–130). Preserve an alternative for people who cannot drag; that accessibility qualification is design-pro synthesis.
- Pull-to-refresh should provide action and progress feedback. The source explicitly treats it as alongside a button and compatible with automatic refresh (pp. 131–132), not a replacement for every refresh route.

## Contradictions and evidence limits

The discoverable-controls section actually celebrates invisible gestures and claims other controls should not exist (pp. 38–44). Its screenshots show context actions, background editing and a radial menu, but do not prove discoverability. Reject the gesture-only prescription; retain shortcuts with usable alternatives and verify first-use success. This also conflicts with the source's own context-dependent framing.

Many examples repeat products seen in [M04](M04.md); this is overlapping illustration, not independent usability evidence. Trend forecasts, claims of universal intuitiveness and predictions of ubiquitous social features are historical commentary. Patent descriptions in the gesture opening (pp. 11–13) are not evidence that a shipping platform implemented every gesture. The Snapchat/Messenger page has Pinterest photo credits (p. 43), a source attribution inconsistency. Static examples cannot establish keyboard, screen-reader, error or animation behavior.

## Demo applications

For the chatbot, preserve conversation position, keep the composer visible, tie failure and retry to the affected turn, and make the empty conversation actionable. The source supports continuity, input selection and empty-state framing; the exact response-progress/error flow is design-pro synthesis to test. For the game page, expose release/action information directly rather than importing app drawers or social feeds merely because the catalog includes them.
