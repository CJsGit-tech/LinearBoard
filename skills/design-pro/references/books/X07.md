# X07 — Idiot Buttons: The Placebo in UX Design

**Source:** Oliver McGough, identified as marketer/UXer at Usabilla (pp. 1, 3), *Idiot Buttons: The Placebo in UX Design*. Original filename: `User Experience/Idiot Buttons - The Placebo in UX Design.pdf`. No publication date is established. Locators are PDF pages; contents entries are approximate (the save discussion starts on p. 13, personalization on p. 15).

## The need beneath the proposed technique

The essay argues that automation can leave people uncertain about control, freshness and whether their work was saved (pp. 3–14). Those are useful design questions. A person who has repeatedly lost work may need clear, trustworthy persistence feedback even when saving is automatic. An automatic feed may still need a real on-demand refresh for a time-sensitive task.

The traffic-button, card-dealing and illusion-of-control stories (pp. 4–9) illustrate the author's argument, but the PDF does not provide enough primary evidence to generalize from them to every interface. Its claimed proportion of inactive New York crossing buttons is historical and unverified here. Real crossing controls may have functions not visible to an observer; do not infer that a button is inert merely because the lights do not change immediately.

## Deliberate rejection of deceptive controls

The author explicitly endorses false/no-op interactions to reassure users (pp. 10, 13–14, 17). **Design-pro does not adopt that prescription.** A control should perform the action its label and feedback imply. Preserve familiarity through real behavior or accurate status, rather than simulating a save that never checks or persists data. This is a deliberate design-pro ethical and usability judgment, not a recommendation made by the source.

For refresh (pp. 11–12), distinguish rechecking and finding no new content from doing nothing. A genuine request may legitimately return the same records. Show that a check completed or that data could not be refreshed; do not imply new data or successful connectivity when neither was established. The source itself acknowledges that some manual refreshes actually fetch on demand, despite calling them “soft placebos.”

For saving (pp. 13–14), distinguish local changes, a pending write, a completed write and failure. An explicit Save can commit/check pending work or expose accurate persistence status. Do not promise durable or cloud storage from a purely visual confirmation. These alternatives are design-pro synthesis.

## Reject invented effort and certainty

The personalized-feed example asserts that Quora's wait is filler and speculates that computation probably takes microseconds (pp. 15–16). The PDF shows a loading screen but no trace, implementation evidence or measurement supporting that claim. Do not turn the speculation into a fact about the product.

Do not add fake progress or artificial waiting to imply personalization, computation or value. Show actual progress where known, and use an indeterminate state when completion cannot be estimated. A local simulated chatbot may demonstrate a pending reply with explicit demo disclosure; it must not imply that a live model, network or expensive algorithm is working. These are design-pro applications, contrary to the essay's recommended anticipation tactic.

## Visual and evidence scope

The pedestrian signal and button imagery (pp. 4–5), refresh example (p. 11), save illustration (p. 13) and interest-selection/loading example (p. 15) show contexts and interface presentations. They do not establish backend behavior, user benefit, durability or causal psychological effects. The closing promotion (p. 18) is a vendor advertisement.

This is an opinion essay offering manipulative techniques, with anecdotes and secondary psychological framing. Retain the underlying problems—uncertainty and loss of meaningful control—while solving them through honest state, actual actions and tested recovery. Do not use the source's insulting terminology for users in product copy or research.
