# X11 — The Field Guide to UX Strategy

**Source:** Robert Hoekman Jr., *The Field Guide to UX Strategy: Turning Vision Into Action*, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 1–2). Original filename: `User Experience/The Field Guide to UX Strategy.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match.

## A strategy should make decisions explainable

Research constraints and concerns, then define objectives that guide later choices rather than treating a feature list as the strategy (pp. 6–14). Connect evidence, rationale and action explicitly; the deposit-frequency flow and ice-cream/cookie analogy illustrate missing logical links (p. 13). They do not establish causality simply because a persuasive explanation exists.

Keep the working strategy short enough to use, with vision, circumstances of use, product-specific design criteria and success measures (pp. 10–12, 44–52). The p. 11 template shows vision, situation and criteria; later text adds metrics. A two-sided sheet, slide deck and printed posters are the author's dissemination preferences, not mandatory artifacts. Scale documentation to the decision and preserve deeper evidence separately.

## Discovery should start with what is already known

Inspect the existing product, research and history before proposing another solution (pp. 17–27). Ask why the product exists, what stage it is in, what was tried, what worked or failed, what outcome is wanted and what constraints affect it. Identify relevant responsibilities and feasible communication. This supports reusing supplied context rather than repeating an interview.

Make outcomes concrete and consider side effects: more signups may accompany lower retention; more page views may merely mean fragmented content (pp. 22–23). The example percentages are placeholders, not recommended targets. A larger metric is not automatically better.

Stakeholders, users, beta testers, subject experts, competitor reviews and recorded behavior reveal different things (pp. 28–41). Ask people about areas they know, follow up on actual situations and compare sources. Do not assume a stakeholder's revenue concern explains every answer. Recruiting only enthusiastic active users misses people who left or cannot use the product. Three/five participants, no compensation and an assumption that beta products need replacing are author heuristics, not universal research rules (pp. 33–36).

When evidence is absent, a provisional strategy can still guide exploration (p. 41); mark it as provisional. This qualifies the earlier claim that every strategic element has already been researched and vetted (p. 8).

## Specific criteria and meaningful measures

Describe what the product helps people do and under what circumstances before prescribing the interface (pp. 45–50). Prefer an actionable criterion, such as exposing relevant trends and outliers in an analytics task, to unsupported “fast, easy, intuitive” language (pp. 47–49). The insurance demographics are one historical example, not a general persona or an appropriate default for the demos.

The author's criticism of decorative persona detail can improve focus on tasks, but abilities, experience and access needs still affect the design. This differs from persona-first framing in [X06](X06.md#link-people-situations-and-paths). The source's own sample still includes demographic assumptions. Its instruction to hide every inactive feature and generous spacing everywhere is a product-specific design choice, not a reusable universal rule (pp. 49–50). The accompanying cortisol/adrenaline explanation lacks sufficient evidence here and is not adopted as a physiological design guarantee.

Select a small set of measures tied to goals; HEART is presented as one organizing model (pp. 50–52). Not every design concern can be reduced to increasing/decreasing a number, and these commercial examples do not exhaust public or personal product goals.

## Validation must permit disagreement

Revisit interpretation with the people who supplied evidence and ask experts to scrutinize domain assumptions (pp. 56–65). Explain how a proposed change relates to an earlier concern, then observe the relevant task where possible. An approving response to a presentation is not task validation.

**Design-pro rejects the chapter's recommendation to suppress uncertainty or strong reactions through a polished, authoritative presentation** (pp. 57–62). Team agreement does not prove a strategy has no serious error. Do not defer all meaningful criticism until after launch or describe skeptical participants as enemies. Confidence should coexist with explicit assumptions, falsifiable expectations and willingness to revise. This is a deliberate qualification of the source.

Live experiments may supply stronger behavioral evidence for some questions (pp. 64–65), but only within the authorized scope and appropriate risk controls. The book does not authorize publishing, contacting users or adding tracking for these local demos.

## Core flows and proportionate fidelity

Use the core task flows to establish an interaction language and communicate changes as the work evolves (pp. 66–81). Sketches, wireframes, clickable prototypes and code answer different questions. The checkout illustrations show structural emphasis and transitions (pp. 73–75). Hand icons can guide a demonstration, but the source explicitly removes them for unguided usability testing (p. 75).

Prototype code is not automatically production-ready, but neither is it universally unusable or slower than every design tool (pp. 75–76). Choose the format supported by the environment and the uncertainty. Preserve relevant changes and rationale rather than posting every file by habit or requiring a separate visual designer for every task.

Rapidly revising between participant sessions can expose obvious problems (pp. 78–79). Record which version each person saw; five sessions with changing designs do not create a comparable fixed-design sample or prove all testing can stop that day. The claim that no further testing is needed is rejected. Confirm the changed behavior and remaining risks before expanding a completion claim.

## Data needs context and provenance

Start from useful success measures, preserve denominators and examine outcomes over time alongside qualitative evidence (pp. 82–91). A higher signup rate can hide poorer-fit signups; lower time on a page can reflect faster completion rather than lost interest. Retention/cohort and analytics screenshots illustrate different views (pp. 51, 84–85); they do not establish a current vendor capability or success threshold.

The author eventually qualifies “numbers demonstrate truth”: analytics show some actions, not why, and should be interpreted with user evidence (pp. 85–90). They can also be missing, incorrectly instrumented or biased. Percentages need counts and cohort/time definitions; absolute volumes can matter too. Do not lower negative feedback by suppressing opportunities to report it.

A week-long test, 25% conversion rate and the Atlassian percentage changes are historical anecdotes without enough methods here for general forecasts (pp. 87–89). Waiting seven days is not a universal sample-size/stopping rule, nor a reason to ignore a serious regression. The guide's support for informed revision is useful; its blanket surveillance and revenue-centered rhetoric are not sufficient justification for data collection.

## Reusable application

For `init`, connect intended outcome, actual setting, known evidence, constraints and a small set of design criteria. For `context`, reconcile changed assumptions and affected decisions. For the game and chat demos, use this short rationale to explain primary flows and what their local tests can establish. Keep project facts outside the reusable skill. This application serves the source's practical intent without importing its adversarial leadership style or fixed process rules.
