# P04 — The Guide to Wireframing

**Source:** UXPin; introduction signed Chris Bank (PDF p. 4). Exact filename: `Wireframes, Mockups, Prototypes/The Guide to Wireframing.pdf`. Full text pp. 1–114 read and all 114 originals inspected. Examples explicitly discuss 2013–2014 trends (p. 70); that does not establish a publication date. Most body folios match PDF pages, but original p. 4 says 3, and the contents incorrectly point to Fresh Patterns p. 71, Principles p. 105 and About UXPin p. 118 (actual chapter starts PDF 70, 102 and closing promotion 114). References here use PDF page positions. No linked site, prototype or video was executed.

## A communication purpose before a wireframe format

Pp. 5–10 distinguish structure, content, hierarchy, functionality and behavior. A representation should make the needed decisions discussable by designers, developers, PMs and relevant stakeholders. The role examples are possibilities, not rules that backend developers ignore content, UX designers ignore hierarchy, or external partners always need polished work. The Yelp annotations pp. 6–7 and mobile-frame editor p. 8 illustrate documentation, not verified application behavior.

Pp. 11–24 describe medium, fidelity and annotations as separable choices. Text length, selected colors, actual images and shown interactions can be realistic even if the surrounding draft remains rough (pp. 17–21). A grayscale draft alone cannot prove a color-dependent state, and a pictured interaction is not an executed interaction. Original p. 17 shows Menu/Carousel/three boxes; p. 23 shows create→publish→consume routes around a slideshow. Use a route/state description when one screen cannot explain the task. P. 22 proposes notes for trigger/function/content/behavior/constraints; note actions, destinations, relevant conditions and unanswered questions without pretending annotations eliminate scope changes.

The claim that every team must wireframe or else produce a poor MVP (p. 12) conflicts with the context-sensitive minimum detail on pp. 21–22 and explicit no-recreation advice p. 113. Retain the latter: reuse a sufficient existing artifact, direct code experiment or known component when it already resolves the question. An interactive wireframe is a kind of prototype; the book's claim it is only near a prototype or only possible in certain software categories (p. 23) is not a technical boundary. It does not equal a shippable MVP.

## Media and tool tradeoffs

Pp. 25–35 cover sketches, boards, cutouts and stencils. Quick notation, rearrangeable pieces and a shared overview can help exploration. The original mobile cutout p. 32 illustrates manually changed content; it does not reproduce native keyboard, timing or access behavior. Reject categorical claims that paper cannot be dated, copied, versioned, shared or linked: those require deliberate capture/organization, rather than being impossible (pp. 28–29). A whiteboard in a hallway also does not guarantee access, comprehension or appropriate visibility. Sketch usefulness depends on communicable meaning, not art-school training (p. 29). Stencils do not necessarily become useless with every platform release, and software libraries do not automatically remain current (p. 34).

Pp. 36–48 compare word processing, graphics, presentations and specialized tools. Familiarity, editable reusable elements, actual interaction/flow capability, collaboration, export and revision effort are useful criteria. Reject everyone-knows-Word/PowerPoint assumptions, only-two-tools framing, speculative timed superiority and compulsory simultaneous editing/cloud storage. Historical feature/no-feature comparisons, market claims, Sketch SVG limits and UXPin's supposedly unmatched total package were not verified as current and are not adopted. P. 40 says graphic tools cannot export images to slides despite its own export/PDF discussion; do not repeat that absolute. Existing code/components may be the most practical local prototype. No paid/cloud service is required by the two demos.

## Libraries are hypotheses and reusable starting points

Pp. 49–69 show historical importable and standalone collections: Patterns by UXPin, Mockups To Go, I Love Wireframes, Wireframe Showcase, Web Without Words, Wireframes Magazine, PatternTap, UX Archive, Behance, Dribbble, search/Pinterest, UI Patterns, Pttrns, Android App Patterns, Inspired UI, Mobile Patterns and MOObileFrames. The original screenshots include music list/player/overflow variants (p. 51), full-page/control collections (p. 52), stripped layouts (p. 55), task-indexed flows (p. 58) and platform/category filters (pp. 64–67).

Distinguish isolated attractive screens from a flow, documented pattern rationale and an editable implementation. Removing content can reveal layout but cannot validate label comprehension or real reading. Source counts, freshness, rankings, monthly traffic, free offers and import support are historical assertions; none determines suitability or present availability. The source itself warns seeing is not understanding (p. 68). Reuse the underlying problem/decision when suitable; do not copy assets or assume a displayed gallery grants an asset license.

## Historical pattern gallery, with action semantics

Pp. 70–101 are observations and practitioner preferences, substantially overlapping the library's earlier mobile/web pattern examples. They are not independent tests or current product claims. Key lessons and qualifications:

- **Navigation pp. 72–80:** fixed navigation may reduce travel but is not universal or always preferable; drawers do not remove the need to prioritize information architecture. Hidden hold/swipe/header actions need discoverability and alternatives when appropriate. Original p. 72 shows marketing webpages, not app scroll behavior; p. 73's image is a receipt/rating screen, not direct evidence for Facebook/Mailbox drawer claims. Tinder's broad/detail/photo continuity p. 75 concerns state retention, not responsive CSS or automatically intuitive behavior. The Uber selector/map pp. 76, RelateIQ hold menu and Snapchat header count p. 77, Secret menu p. 78 and LinkedIn drawer p. 79 show selected states only. No user or runtime test was performed.
- **Content pp. 81–84:** masonry cards can fit browsing; shape does not establish responsive/accessibility quality. Maps are useful when location is central, not mandatory decorative backgrounds for every local service. Circular avatars are optional; the source acknowledges its unclear advantage. Full-bleed photos are not measured engagement or reliable text contrast.
- **Actions pp. 85–92:** original p. 85 distinguishes Pinterest's dismissing an open Add menu from Spotify's Unfollow this playlist. Neither is generic cancellation of every action. Original Mailbox p. 88 shows a checkmark state and scheduling choices; do not equate archive/completion, read status and deletion from this image. Acquisition value is not causal evidence of gesture efficiency. Double-tap, swipe and drag are optional shortcuts, not sufficient controls. Reject p. 92's insistence that hidden photo gestures should have no other controls. Unlike is not Dislike; consistent direction and an understandable recovery path matter. The Messenger p. 90 dialog explicitly separates Delete Conversation, Archive Conversation and Cancel; do not collapse their consequences. Its names are historical product UI, not an authorization to delete anything.
- **Social pp. 93–100:** friend lists, following, feeds, conversation lists/threads, share sheets, rating and groups are options for actual tasks. A private-looking chat does not establish confidentiality or consent to share financial transactions. The YouTube p. 98 pair changes Subscribe/Subscribed, so it is not direct visual evidence of the prose's binary Like/Dislike claim. Binary ratings are not universally superior. Venmo's contact/email lists p. 100 are not authority to access contacts or send invitations. Forecasts that everyone gains millions of friends or all products need social features remain unsupported.

**Synthesis:** name each action by the actual user intent and resulting state, keep related control/content states aligned, and verify navigation/recovery rather than copying a fashionable gesture. The chatbot can use clear conversation navigation and a labeled retry; it need not acquire a social graph or hidden swipe vocabulary. The game page does not need a feed, map, rating or contact upload because the gallery contains them.

## Discovery, consistency and feasible scope

Pp. 102–113 support an economical brief: users, tasks/needs, business outcome, existing patterns, gaps, requirements and constraints (p. 103); success measure and process expectations (p. 104); actual evidence rather than designer preference (p. 105). These are prompts to adapt, not a questionnaire quota or an OKR ceremony. The book's speculative OKR ancestry and reductive generalization about the average person's life are not design evidence. Understand a requested feature's underlying task without dismissing explicit user requirements.

Pp. 106–109 favor scope focus, change tied to improvement and a consistent visual vocabulary with meaningful variants. Two–three variations per attribute is a practitioner heuristic, not a cap on required statuses. Less functionality does not automatically mean better measurement, lower cost or adequate coverage. Glasses' shape/size may affect function, not merely decoration; preserve the functional meaning of visual decisions. A new iteration need not be novel to be useful.

Pp. 110–111 explicitly retain realistic content: three versus six lines, heading wrapping, image orientation and numeric input materially affect layout. Test representative and challenging content in the actual language and viewport. Iteration can reduce uncertainty but does not guarantee small failures or prevent major ones. P. 112's drawing-always-faster-than-code and no-small-changes claims are not measurable rules. Choose appropriate effort and actual acceptance checks; shipped-is-better-than-perfect does not authorize ignoring known task failures. Reuse a sufficient sketch/reference rather than recreating deliverables for ceremony (p. 113). P. 114 is vendor promotion, not independent evidence.
