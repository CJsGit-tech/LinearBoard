# U01 — Clever Interactive Techniques for Web Storytelling

**Source:** Jerry Cao and Cameron Chapman, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 3–4, 7). Full title on pp. 1–2; original filename is truncated: `User Interface/Clever Interactive Techniques For Web Storytell.pdf`. Locators are PDF pages; body folios match. Page 27 is an image-only vendor advertisement, not missing/unreadable text.

## Choose interaction only when it serves the story and task

The guide explicitly begins and ends by saying that visual storytelling need not be interactive (pp. 6–7, 26). A single image may be enough; a complex story may require more. Choose the representation for what must be understood rather than an arbitrary image count. An information-retrieval task should not be delayed by a drawn-out narrative. Wikipedia is a contextual example, not proof that information services never benefit from useful interactive explanations.

Involving the person can make information relevant: the Slavery Footprint questionnaire and Tesla's direct questions illustrate different approaches (pp. 8–10). Do not guarantee investment or enjoyment merely because someone supplies input or sees personalized language. Any calculated result needs an explainable basis and limits. The Tesla example describes a scroll-driven progression from battery to road trips to fuel savings; it is a historical description, not an independently tested conversion result.

## Rewards and progress need a meaningful purpose

Gamification is presented through risk/cost and reward, visual feedback, Foursquare/Swarm and Dropbox onboarding/storage incentives (pp. 11–14). The cue–routine–reward diagram is an explanatory model, not proof that every repeated action is a habit. The Dropbox checklist illustrates progress and optional incentives; its exact storage amount, seven tasks and claimed growth effect are historical, not current service terms or an effect size for another product.

The author's praise of check-ins and rewards differs from the concern about arbitrary points in [X16](X16.md). Keep that disagreement visible. Rewards can add effort or pressure; more check-ins/messages are not automatically more useful. A trial extension for social sharing, shown on p. 11, is not a reason for design-pro to post or contact anyone. No incentive mechanism is required for either demo.

## Support common actions without hijacking them

Use meaningful imagery and consistent copy to indicate what a control does and why it matters (pp. 15–18). The Squarespace examples align a product promise with shown output; they do not establish a five-to-seven-word heading as a universal rule. Scroll-triggered changes and parallax may guide sequence but can also obscure content, interfere with orientation or cost performance. Keep essential content and task access usable without decorative motion; this is design-pro qualification.

Humaan uses hover reveals and scroll animations, while Babel's falling cat is tied to scrolling (pp. 17–20). The book acknowledges that a static image cannot show the latter behavior (p. 20). These screenshots demonstrate composition only. Provide keyboard/touch access to required content and preserve an understandable alternative for reduced motion; do not adopt the claim that animation is the only way to prevent copy overload.

## Easter eggs must remain optional

Playful hidden interactions may fit some audiences, but the guide warns they can frustrate others (pp. 21–23). Museum of Mario explicitly invites exploration, while its main history remains accessible without discovering hidden clicks. Keep actions critical to the user's goal visibly discoverable. Rarity alone is not sufficient reason to hide a critical recovery or accessibility control. A playful game site still needs a clear primary action and release/platform information.

## Map the wider journey without inventing a universal story arc

The beginning/middle/end model can help organize a narrative and the portion of a journey served by a site (pp. 24–25). A purchase can complete the site's commercial objective while leaving the person's actual goal unfinished, as in the product-comparison example. Distinguish the user's outcome from the business's conversion event.

The claim that every good story, even one image, follows the same arc is too broad. A complete experience map is optional when the task already has enough context. The empty journey template illustrates a method, not observed behavior or emotion. Align content and interaction with the actual task and explain where the product's responsibility ends.

## Application

For the game-release Web demo, use a coherent introduction to the world/gameplay and a readable release/action section. Optional atmosphere can support it, but important content must not require hunting or watching a forced sequence. For the Mobile chatbot, focus on understandable conversation and feedback; no visual story or hidden gimmick is needed. These are design-pro applications, not tested effectiveness claims.
