# X05 — Designing Better UX With UI Patterns

**Source:** Ivana McConnell, Jerry Cao, Ben Gremillion and Matt Ellis, UXPin, copyright 2015 (pp. 3, 5, 7). Original filename: `User Experience/Designing Better UX with UI Patterns.pdf`. All locators are PDF pages.

## Patterns are hypotheses about familiar problems

Patterns document a recurring solution and can reduce relearning, but their effectiveness depends on the actual users and context (pp. 13–21, 45–48). Familiarity is useful evidence to investigate, not proof that the most popular treatment is best. The opening's popularity/effectiveness feedback-loop claim is stronger than the book's evidence (p. 6).

The UI/UX table connects consistency, instructions, sound, color and customization to possible experience effects (p. 8). It usefully distinguishes a designed input from a person's resulting experience, which a designer does not directly control (pp. 8–10). Do not treat every suggested effect as established: universal color/physiological associations and guaranteed trust or satisfaction need more support. Sounds can also be annoying or unavailable; the table itself notes context.

## Capability, signifier and expectation

The source distinguishes what the interface enables, the cue indicating it, and the user's interpretation (pp. 32–33). Its favorite-heart example illustrates why perceived capability should match actual behavior. An icon's meaning is not guaranteed across people, cultures or products; a clear label may be necessary. Accessible names and input semantics are additional implementation concerns, not established by this visual example.

Hamburger menus, breadcrumbs, account registration, continuous scroll, presence indicators and FAQs solve different access or content problems (pp. 15–18). A breadcrumb supports hierarchical orientation, while a FAQ answers recurring questions; neither is a universal site requirement. The presence example includes words as well as colored status marks. Continuous scroll is an option for suitable browsing, not an automatic choice for every long page.

## Evaluate change rather than copy convention

When departing from an established pattern, ask why, what is improved and whether intended users can learn it (pp. 24–25). Novelty can add expression without making the main task harder. The MailChimp completion illustration is a delight example (pp. 26–27), not a reason to delay an operation, hide feedback or introduce rewards into every product.

The prototype loop identifies the problem, studies relevant existing solutions, makes assumptions explicit, tests and revises (pp. 28–31). Observe behavior as well as comments. Its minimum-five recommendation and coworker hallway fallback do not prove adequate population coverage or statistical sufficiency. The p. 30 diagram depicts iteration rather than a mandatory sequence of tool purchases.

## Choose at several levels

The functional taxonomy includes data interaction, simplification, navigation, incentives, hierarchy and social behavior (pp. 34–37). These are overlapping retrieval categories, not a checklist of features to add.

The second taxonomy distinguishes contextual structure, strategic flow and granular consistency (pp. 38–44). A checkout is a contextual example; choosing how navigation opens is a flow decision; keeping a repeated control's treatment coherent is a consistency decision. It is useful to resolve structure before polishing isolated controls, but the claim that stepwise transactions apply only to ecommerce is too narrow. The sample 400×400px card (p. 44) illustrates documenting a choice, not a responsive sizing rule.

The Vangarde and ThinkBlink menus share an entry/close convention but differ in visible choices, scrolling and expression (pp. 40–42). This shows that consistent meaning allows deliberate presentation variants. Do not infer that an “artistic audience” will necessarily understand or prefer concealed navigation. The carousel and style-guide examples connect repeated treatment with documented choices (pp. 39–40); they overlap the already reviewed style-guide material.

## Compare the mechanism, not merely the screenshot

The pattern-selection case starts with users needing many options without constant clutter (pp. 45–48). It compares hover-revealed icon actions on Pinterest with click-opened word actions on Quora, then proposes a custom combination. The valuable lesson is to analyze trigger, visibility and representation separately. The conclusion that fun/simple icons need no explanation is unproven. Check recognition, names, focus and touch access; do not copy hover-only access. See [M08, p. 13](M08.md#adapt-interaction-not-just-width).

## Unsupported mechanisms and dated resources

The neurodesign section, particularly its two-dopamine-jolts explanation for every recognized/successful pattern (pp. 11–14, 22–23), supplies no primary evidence establishing that causal account. The cue/routine/reward diagram (p. 26) is a simplified model, not a validated equation for interface quality. Design-pro does not claim it can maximize a user's dopamine, diagnose brain responses or infer satisfaction from a familiar icon. Use observable comprehension and task behavior instead.

The suggestion that making a form harder to read can improve data quality is a quoted opinion (p. 9), not a basis to reduce legibility intentionally. The source alternates between invisible UI and visible signifiers; adopt task clarity rather than a universal invisibility goal.

The resource gallery (pp. 49–53) documents historical pattern libraries and includes mobile patterns overlapping [M06](M06.md). Its availability, pricing, current ranking and compatibility have not been verified. Static screenshots of GIF/animation libraries do not demonstrate their motion. Treat its Dark Patterns entry as a reminder to examine consequences, not an endorsement of manipulative patterns. The closing tool promotion (pp. 54–55) is not a required design-pro dependency.
