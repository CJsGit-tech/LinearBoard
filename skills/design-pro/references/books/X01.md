# X01 — 10 Pro Tips to a Smarter UX Design Process

**Source:** Jessica Lowry and Jerry Cao, UXPin, copyright 2015. Original filename: `User Experience/10 Pro Tips to a Smarter UX Design Process.pdf`. All locators are PDF pages.

## A flexible process around an actual problem

Define the problem before selecting a solution, and distinguish symptoms from causes (pp. 4–7). Adapt the process to time, resources and skill rather than requiring an ideal sequence for every task (p. 6). Knowing specific users and use cases helps bound scope (pp. 8–9); do not substitute a demographic persona for evidence about the task.

The book encourages extreme alternatives and inspiration from other domains (pp. 9–11). Use divergent exploration to widen possibilities, then choose against the user's need. Its instruction to break conventions for their own sake is a brainstorming tactic, not a shipping criterion. The Duolingo example shows a game-like learning treatment; the cited 2015 valuation does not demonstrate that its visual choices caused successful learning or business outcomes.

## Hypotheses, critique and evaluation

Establish a testable expectation before prototyping: connect a proposed action, intended outcome and user need (pp. 11–12). Design-pro application: define what observation would support or contradict it; a reassuring rationale alone is not a test.

Gather different perspectives without making design a committee vote (p. 13). A critique can reveal gaps but does not replace testing with intended users. The book's pair-design suggestion (pp. 21–22) supports collaboration, not a requirement to create extra agents or invite stakeholders for every small change.

Paper prototypes and quick walkthroughs help explore sequence before becoming attached to polished pixels (pp. 16–20). Its 3–5-person hallway suggestion (p. 17) is an exploratory tactic, not a universal sample size or proof of representativeness. Choose fidelity for the question: a paper flow can test order and labels but cannot establish software-keyboard or runtime performance behavior.

## Connected, minimal documentation

Link user context, scenarios, flows and UI artifacts so another person can understand why a design exists (pp. 14–16). The persona on p. 14 includes goals and behaviors alongside demographic data. The p. 15 scenario makes a marketer's late-night request and time pressure concrete, then the flow connects signup, loading and onboarding states. This supports recording situations and transitions, not producing a persona merely to fill a template.

Minimize documents that do not advance the decision; avoid polishing one artifact while its relationships remain unclear (p. 16). Shared artifacts and affinity work can connect a problem to evidence and a solution (pp. 18–19). A physical wall is one collaboration medium, not a mandatory deliverable.

The closing process map asks about symptoms, affected users, emotional context, importance and current workarounds; it then groups resources, collaboration, ideation and creation around testing/refinement (p. 22). The diagram explicitly presents suggestions for defining one's own process. It is useful support for adaptive discovery rather than a fixed questionnaire.

## Limits and design-pro application

The source is practitioner advice and vendor illustration, not a controlled demonstration that its complete process improves every project. Historical tool examples and quotations are not verified product guidance or primary evidence. It cross-promotes other UXPin titles; overlapping advice should not be counted as independent validation.

For `init`, reuse known context, ask about the most consequential unknown and produce only useful brief fields. For the demos, record the assumed user/task, a testable design expectation and observations from the actual flow. Those are design-pro applications of pp. 6, 11–12, 14–20; a fictional brief remains an assumption rather than user research.
