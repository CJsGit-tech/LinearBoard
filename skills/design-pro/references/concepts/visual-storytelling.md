# Visual storytelling and optional interaction

Use visual narrative to explain something the audience needs to understand. Interactivity is optional; the useful amount of content/motion depends on the task. [U01, pp. 6–7, 26](../books/U01.md#choose-interaction-only-when-it-serves-the-story-and-task)

## DO

- Connect imagery, copy and sequence to the audience's goal and the product's role in that journey.
- Use a single image or a clear static sequence when it communicates enough.
- Keep critical information/actions visibly discoverable; reserve easter eggs for genuinely optional exploration.
- Use feedback to clarify an actual action or state change rather than adding animation to every scroll. [U01, pp. 15–26](../books/U01.md)

## DON'T

- Do not make release information or the primary action depend on finding a hidden interaction.
- Do not force a lengthy story on a quick information task, or infer that every gamer wants a puzzle-like website.
- Do not use hover-only reveals for required content or obscure the page when decorative motion is unavailable.
- Do not treat a static screenshot as verification of scrolling, animation, input access or increased engagement. [U01, pp. 7, 17–23](../books/U01.md)

## Guidelines and example

Start with what must be understood, then choose a meaningful sequence and representation. A narrative can cover the site's part in a longer journey without pretending a purchase completes the person's ultimate goal. The source's beginning/middle/end model is a planning option, not a universal rule. [U01, pp. 24–25](../books/U01.md#map-the-wider-journey-without-inventing-a-universal-story-arc)

For a game-release page, a concise world/gameplay introduction can lead to platform/release information and a clear next action. Optional atmospheric artwork should not block navigation, reading or the action. For chat, readable messages and honest feedback may be sufficient without any narrative ornament. These are design-pro applications. Check [current motion/auto-update criteria](../current-accessibility.md) where applicable; evaluate input and motion alternatives in the implementation.

## Choose meaningful imagery without effect-size promises

Choose a photo, diagram, illustration or mascot for what it explains, not claims that visuals are always processed 60,000 times faster or guarantee a fixed recall/conversion increase. Cohesion and recognizable differences can help a sequence; a familiar icon still needs an understandable purpose in context. The source's broad claims and its overlap with U01 are qualified in [U08, PDF pp. 7–35, 51–67](../books/U08.md).

Use appropriate [text alternatives](../current-accessibility.md#non-text-content), preserve information without decorative effects, and make audio optional. A static fallback image does not replace meaningful information from video. Historical loop-duration/HD prescriptions are not universal requirements, and the author's praise of one autoplay soundtrack does not establish user preference. [U08, PDF pp. 36–50](../books/U08.md#medium-and-visual-style-follow-the-job); [current audio guidance](../current-accessibility.md#audio-control).

## Choose the scrolling model explicitly

A finite story, an extending feed and scroll-controlled animation solve different problems. Preserve direct access to known information and a person's location where useful. Do not force full-viewport sections or hide essential text until a scroll animation fires; fixed tutorial offsets are not general measurements. The historical galleries illustrate visual options without proving comprehension or engagement. [W03, PDF pp. 134–157](../books/W03.md#separate-scrolling-formats)
