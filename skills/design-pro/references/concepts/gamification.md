# Gamification, motivation and progress

Use game mechanics only when they support an outcome the person already values. Make the task useful first. [X16, pp. 6–17](../books/X16.md#conditions-tradeoffs-and-exceptions)

## DO

- Define the user benefit and what real progress means.
- Show completed and ongoing work accurately, and allow self-set goals where they fit.
- Let people decline competition and rewards, and stop without losing unrelated capabilities.
- Evaluate task quality and unwanted pressure alongside repeated use *(synthesis; corrects the addiction framing in [X16, pp. 15–28](../books/X16.md))*.

## DON'T

- Do not add points, badges, streaks or artificial scarcity by default.
- Do not use time spent, popularity or activity counts as a measure of learning, expertise, health or task completion.
- Do not hide essential controls to create a discovery reward, or interrupt every action with celebration.
- Do not treat external rewards as always helpful or always harmful; test the specific one. [X16, pp. 14–27](../books/X16.md)

## Guidelines and example

Use the trigger/action/reward/investment model to describe a proposed loop. For each repetition, ask what it produces, whether the measure can be gamed and whether the person can leave. A visible "done" area is often enough for task tracking. [X16, pp. 9–10, 22–24](../books/X16.md)

A game-release page can stay a plain page. A chatbot preserves useful work without rewarding extra messages. To study an incentive change, compare task success and reported pressure *(synthesis)*.
